Chords in a circle

Why 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 stops doubling.

The trap

Put n points on a circle and draw every chord between them, with no three chords meeting at one point. Then count the regions inside the circle.

n123456
Regions12481631

One point gives 1 region, two give 2, then 4, 8 and 16. It's hard not to guess 32 for six points. The real answer is 31.

The key idea

Draw the chords one at a time. A new chord adds one region, plus one more for every chord it crosses on the way. So the total is 1, plus one for every chord, plus one for every crossing.

There are n2 chords. A crossing needs two chords with four different endpoints, and any four points on the circle give exactly one crossing: the two diagonals of their quadrilateral. So there are n4 crossings.

The formula

R(n) = 1 + n2 + n4

Check it with six points: 1 + 15 + 15 = 31.

So why did it look like doubling? By Pascal's rule, 1 + n2 + n4 adds up the first five entries of row n − 1 of Pascal's triangle. For n ≤ 5 that's the whole row, which sums to 2n−1. At n = 6 the row has six entries, so the sum misses the last one: 32 − 1 = 31.

Frequently asked questions

The essentials about the competition path, how the Guide works, and how to get involved.

contact@usamoguide.com
What are AMC, AIME, and USAMO?

These are the big rungs of the US math competition ladder. AMC 8 is for middle schoolers and AMC 10/12 is for high schoolers, and both are 25-question multiple-choice contests. Score well enough and you qualify for the AIME, a much harder 15-question exam with numerical answers. Do well on the AIME too and you're in USAMO (or USAJMO) territory, probably the toughest math test most high schoolers will ever take. For official dates and registration, check the MAA competitions page.

Is this an official syllabus?

Nope. USAMO Guide is built by the community, for the community. It's our best attempt at organizing what actually works for AMC, AIME, and USAMO prep, and it isn't blessed by the MAA :/ Think of it as notes passed down and refined by people who've been through the process. It keeps getting better as more people chip in.

I found a bug, typo, or confusing explanation. What do I do?

Tell us! Email contact@usamoguide.com. If you're comfortable with GitHub, you can also open an issue on our repository, which is often the fastest way to get something fixed.

I want live classes or one-on-one tutoring.

That's not really what we do. We're a self-paced resource, and AoPS runs solid online classes if you want live instruction. If you still want structure and other people around, join one of our study groups or hop into a weekly mock contest to practice under real conditions.

Do I need to be good at math, or qualified for USAMO, to use this?

Not even close. Start on day one of AMC 8 prep or show up already grinding toward USAMO. Either way there's a place for you here. The material ramps from the basics up to olympiad level, so you can jump in wherever you actually are.

Where can I get help when I'm stuck?

Our Discord is the best place. People are usually around to help with a specific problem or concept. Beyond that, you can join a study group, get paired with a mentor, or email us if it's a question about the Guide itself.

How can I contribute?

Please do. Fix a typo, rewrite a confusing explanation, add a problem, improve a diagram, or clean up some code. It all helps. Our GitHub has contribution guidelines and open issues.

Is this open source?

Yes, all of it! Fork it, build on it, poke around and see how it works. Nothing's hidden. Attribution is required, and commercial use isn't allowed.
