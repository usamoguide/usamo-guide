# Introduction

Up to this point, we've differentiated functions by isolating for $y$ and then taking the derivative of the other side. But it's not always easy — or even possible — to solve directly for $y$. This is where **implicit differentiation** comes in.

The idea is to differentiate both sides of an equation with respect to $x$ and apply the chain rule as needed. This works because differentiation is an operation just like addition or subtraction: if you differentiate both sides of a true equation, it remains true.

The key is that whenever you differentiate a term involving $y$, you must multiply by $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ (also written $y'$) via the chain rule, since $y$ is itself a function of $x$.

# Examples

> **Example 1:** Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ given $x^2 + y^2 = 1$.
>
> **Solution:** Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$:
>
> $$\frac{d}{dx}(x^2 + y^2) = \frac{d}{dx}(1)$$
>
> $$2x + 2y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = 0$$
>
> Solving for $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$:
>
> $$\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{-x}{y}}$$
>
> In the second step, we used the chain rule on $y^2$: since $y$ is a function of $x$, differentiating gives $2y \cdot \dfrac{dy}{dx}$.

> **Example 2:** Find $y'$ given $x^2 + xy + y^3 = 0$.
>
> **Solution:** The middle term $xy$ is a product of two functions of $x$, so we need both the product rule and the chain rule. Differentiating both sides:
>
> $$\frac{d}{dx}(x^2 + xy + y^3) = \frac{d}{dx}(0)$$
>
> $$2x + (x \cdot y' + 1 \cdot y) + 3y^2 y' = 0$$
>
> Collecting all $y'$ terms on one side:
>
> $$y'(x + 3y^2) = -2x - y$$
>
> $$\boxed{y' = \frac{-2x - y}{x + 3y^2}}$$

> **Example 3:** Your goal is to find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ at the point $(1,1)$ if $x^y = y^x$ for $x, y > 0$.
>
> **Solution:** 
>
> * Simply take ln of both sides $y \ln x = x \ln y$
>
> * Differentiate implicitly as follows: $y' \ln x + \frac{y}{x} = \ln y + \frac{x}{y} y'$
>
> * Substitute $(x,y) = (1,1)$: $y'(0) + 1 = 0 + 1(y')$
>
> * And boom! $\boxed{y' = 1}$

> **Example 4:** Your goal is to find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ in terms of $x$ and $y$ for the curve defined by $\sin(x+y) = y^2 \cos x$.
>
> **Solution:** 
>
> * First, we differentiate to get $\cos(x+y)(1 + y') = 2y y' \cos x - y^2 \sin x$
>
> * Expanding arrives us at $\cos(x+y) + y'\cos(x+y) = 2y y' \cos x - y^2 \sin x$
>
> * Grouping $y'$ terms gives us $y'[\cos(x+y) - 2y \cos x] = -y^2 \sin x - \cos(x+y)$
>
> * And isolating $y'$ leads us to our final answer: $\boxed{y' = \frac{y^2 \sin x + \cos(x+y)}{2y \cos x - \cos(x+y)}}$

> **Example 5:** Your goal is to find the value of $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ at the point $(0,0)$ for the curve $e^{x+y} = x - y + 1$.
>
> **Solution:** 
>
> * First, we will find the first derivative $e^{x+y}(1 + y') = 1 - y'$
>
> * At $(0,0)$: $e^0(1 + y') = 1 - y' \implies 1 + y' = 1 - y' \implies y' = 0$
>
> * Now, we take the second derivative to get $e^{x+y}(1+y')^2 + e^{x+y}(y'') = -y''$
>
> * Substituting $(0,0)$ and $y'=0$: $1(1+0)^2 + 1(y'') = -y''$
>
> * And so our answer is: $1 + y'' = -y'' \implies \boxed{y'' = -\frac{1}{2}}$

> **Example 6:** Your goal is to find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ at the point $(1,2)$ for the curve $x^3 + y^3 = 9xy$.
>
> **Solution:** 
>
> * First step like usual, we differentiate $3x^2 + 3y^2 y' = 9y + 9xy'$
>
> * Then, we simplify the expression $x^2 + y^2 y' = 3y + 3xy'$
>
> * After that, we will simply substitue $(1,2)$: $1^2 + 2^2 y' = 3(2) + 3(1)y'$
>
> * And solve to get $1 + 4y' = 6 + 3y' \implies \boxed{y' = 5}$

> **Example 7:** If $\tan^{-1}(x^2 y) = x + y^3$, your goal is to find (if it exists) $\frac{dy}{dx}$ at the origin $(0,0)$.
>
> **Solution:** 
>
> * As usual, we differentiate $\frac{1}{1 + (x^2 y)^2} \cdot (2xy + x^2 y') = 1 + 3y^2 y'$
>
> * Substitute $(0,0)$: $\frac{1}{1 + 0} \cdot (0 + 0) = 1 + 3(0)y'$
>
> * And we get something fishy: $\boxed{0 = 1}$

> **Example 8:** Your goal is to find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ at $(1, e)$ for the graph $y \ln y - x^2 e^{x-1} = 0$.
>
> **Solution:** 
>
> * Differentiate like usual to get $(1 \cdot \ln y + y \cdot \frac{1}{y})y' - (2x e^{x-1} + x^2 e^{x-1}) = 0$
>
> * Then, we simplify $(\ln y + 1)y' - e^{x-1}(2x + x^2) = 0$
>
> * Substitute $(1,e)$: $(\ln e + 1)y' - e^0(2(1) + 1^2) = 0$
>
> * And we solve to get: $2y' - 3 = 0 \implies \boxed{y' = \frac{3}{2}}$



# Practice Problems

> **Problem 1:** Given $2xy + x^3 - 3y^2 = 5$, find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$.
>
> **Answer:** $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{-2y - 3x^2}{2x - 6y}$

> **Problem 2:** Given $x^3 y + y^2 - x^2 = 5$, find the slope of the tangent line at the point $(2, 1)$.
>
> **Answer:** $-\dfrac{4}{5}$

> **Problem 3:** Given $\dfrac{1}{x} + \dfrac{1}{y} = \dfrac{1}{12}$, find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$.
>
> **Answer:** $-\dfrac{y^2}{x^2}$
