## The Intuition Prime Factor and Exponent Methods
One of the most fundamental rules of arithmetic guarantees that every nonzero positive integer (excluding 1) can be uniquely
factored into a product of prime numbers and their powers. 

This is very often the starting point of working on a math competition problem because you dig into the core components of elements you are working with to better understand them.

## Formal Definitions and Notation
The defintion of the **fundamental theorem of arithmetic** is:

Let $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ be an integer. Its prime factorization is uniquely given by:

$$
n = p_1^{e_1} p_2^{e_2} \cdots p_k^{e_k} = \prod_{i=1}^k p_i^{e_i}
$$

where $p_1 < p_2 < \dots < p_k$ are prime numbers, and $e_i \ge 1$ are positive integer exponents.

Another basic function that you should know is one for counting the amount of divisors it has by choosing every possible combination of prime factors; for the integer defined as $n = p_1^{e_1} p_2^{e_2} \cdots p_k^{e_k}$:

**Number of Divisors $d(n)$**: The total count of positive factors is found by choosing an exponent from $0$ to $e_i$ for each prime:
$$
d(n) = (e_1 + 1)(e_2 + 1)\cdots(e_k + 1)
$$

Aswell as in similar manner 

**Sum of Divisors $\sigma(n)$**: 
The sum of all positive factors expands into:
$$
\sigma(n) = (1 + p_1 + \dots + p_1^{e_1})(1 + p_2 + \dots + 
p_2^{e_2}) \cdots (1 + p_k + \dots + p_k^{e_k})
$$

Other quick rules you should familiarize with are:

**Perfect Squares**: An integer $n$ is a perfect square if and only if every prime exponent $e_i$ in its prime factorization is even.

The same rule holds for cubes and so on but with their respective power divisiblity.

**GCD and LCM via Prime Factorization**: 

$$
\gcd(a, b) = \prod p_i^{\min(e_i, f_i)}
$$
$$
\operatorname{lcm}(a, b) = \prod p_i^{\max(e_i, f_i)}
$$
Where $a = \prod p_i^{e_i}$ and $b = \prod p_i^{f_i}$

## Scaffolded Examples

### Direct Application (Easy)

Find the total number of positive divisors and the sum of positive divisors of $n = 180$.

**Solution:**

First, find prime factorization of $180$ manually

$$
180 = 18 \cdot 10 = (2 \cdot 3^2) \cdot (2 \cdot 5) = 2^2 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5^1
$$
Using the divisor counting formula:
$$
d(n) = (e_1 + 1)(e_2 + 1)(e_3 + 1)= d(180) = (2 + 1)(2 + 1)(1 + 1) = 18
$$
Using the sum of divisors formula:

$$
\sigma(n) = (1 + 2 + 2^2)(1 + 3 + 3^2)(1 + 5) = 546
$$
So our answers are:
$$
\boxed{18 \text{ and } 546}
$$
respectively.

### Slightly Harder Problem (Medium)
Find all positive integers $n$ such that $2^n + 121$ is a perfect square.

**Solution:**

Suppose $2^n + 121 = m^2$ for some positive integer $m$.

 Rearranging gives a difference of squares:
 $$
 m^2 - 121 = 2^n \implies (m - 11)(m + 11) = 2^n
 $$
 By unique prime factorization, any factor of a power of $2$ must itself be a power of $2$. 

 Let WLOG where $a < b$ and $a + b = n$:
 $$
 m - 11 = 2^a \quad \text{and} \quad m + 11 = 2^b
 $$
Subtracting the two equations eliminates $m$:
$$
(m + 11) - (m - 11) = 2^b - 2^a \implies 22 = 2^a(2^{b-a} - 1) = 2 \cdot 11
$$
And since $b > a$, we know  $2^{b-a} - 1$ must be an odd integer. 
Comparing the even power of $2$ and odd factors on both sides:
$$
2^a = 2 \implies 2^{b-1} - 1 = 11 \implies 2^{b-1} = 12
$$
Since $12$ is not a power of $2$, there are no integer solutions for $b$. Thus, there are:
$$
\boxed{\text{No solutions}}
$$

### Olympiad Application (Hard)

Find all pairs of positive integers $(a, b)$ such that $a^2b + a + b$ is divisible by $ab^2 + b + 1$.

**Solution:**

We start by first establishing that $b$ and $ab^2 + b + 1$ are coprime.

Suppose $d = \gcd(b, ab^2 + b + 1)$. Then $d \mid b$, which implies $d \mid b(ab + 1)$, so $d \mid (ab^2 + b)$.

Since $d$ must also divide $ab^2 + b + 1$, it must divide their difference:
$$
d \mid (ab^2 + b + 1) - (ab^2 + b) \implies d \mid 1
$$
Thus, $\gcd(b, ab^2 + b + 1) = 1$.

Because $b$ shares no common prime factors with the divisor $ab^2 + b + 1$, multiplying the numerator in the given valid fraction $$ab^2 + b + 1 \mid a^2b + a + b$$ by $b$ creates an equivalent divisibility relation without introducing extraneous solutions:
$$
ab^2 + b + 1 \mid a^2b + a + b \iff ab^2 + b + 1 \mid b(a^2b + a + b)
$$
Working out gives:
$$
ab^2 + b + 1 \mid a(ab^2 + b + 1) + (b^2 - a) \implies ab^2 + b + 1 \mid b^2 - a
$$

We now analyze the possible values of $b^2 - a$:

**Case 1:** $b^2 - a = 0$

$\implies a = b^2$;
Testing $a = b^2$ in the original expression:
$$
a^2b + a + b = b^5 + b^2 + b = b(b^4 + b + 1)
$$
and
$$
ab^2 + b + 1 = b^4 + b + 1
$$
Since $b^4 + b + 1 \mid b(b^4 + b + 1)$ holds for all positive integers $b$, $(b^2, b)$ is a valid solution family.
 
**Case 2:** $b^2 - a > 0$

For a positive integer to be divisible by another, the dividend must be at least as large as the divisor:
$$
b^2 - a \ge ab^2 + b + 1
$$
However, since $a \ge 1$ and $b \ge 1$, we have $ab^2 + b + 1 > b^2 > b^2 - a$, creating a contradiction.
 
**Case 3:** $b^2 - a < 0$

Let $a - b^2 > 0$ for the sake of contradiction.
Divisibility requires:
$$
a - b^2 \ge ab^2 + b + 1 \implies a(1 - b^2) \ge b^2 + b + 1
$$
Since $b \ge 1$, we have $(1 - b^2) \le 0$, while $b^2 + b + 1 > 0$. 

A non-positive quantity cannot be greater than or equal to a positive quantity, so no solutions exist here.

Thus, the complete set of solutions is:
$$
\boxed{(b^2, b) \text{ for any strict positive integer } b }
$$

## Reference Guide

Exponent manipulation and prime decomposition allow you to reduce high-degree polynomial expressions and complex divisibility statements down to fundamental properties of prime integer factors.

Important usages are Prime Factorization Identities: 

-**Divisor Count** and **Divisor sum** as seen above.

What is also useful are Algebraic & Bounding Techniques:

-**Algebraic Factorization** like difference of perfect squares can reveal underlying structures formed by prime factors.

-**Coprime Component Splitting:** If $X \cdot Y = p^k$ and $\gcd(X, Y) = 1$, then one factor must be $1$ and the other $p^k$.

-**Size Bounds:** Use divisibility size inequalities ($A \mid B \implies \vert{}A\vert{} \le \vert{}B\vert{}$ for $B \neq 0$) to eliminate impossible exponent ranges.
