## Overview

This lesson is the first of learning about all the amazing properties of circles. We will discussing an important topic known as power of a point. Circles provide unique angle properties that we must capitalize on which in turn allows us to discover similar triangles and ultimately length chase through the concept of power of a point.

Power of a point is a key strategy when working with circles, lines, and points that shows up often in many problems. It is often applicable when multiple lines sharing one point intersect a circle, such as secants, tangents, or intersecting chords. The shared point may be inside or outside the circle, each yielding different formulas. In particular, the key idea is that certain products of segment lengths are equal, allowing you to turn a geometric setup into a simple algebra equation and quickly find missing lengths.

## Power of a Point

### Definitions

Throughout this lesson and generally the chapter, we will reference the following terms a lot.

- A **chord** is a line segment with both endpoints on the circle.
- A **secant line** is a line that intersects a circle at two points.
- A **tangent line** is a line that touches a circle at exactly one point. When a radius is drawn from the center to this point, it will be perpendicular to the original tangent line.

### The Theorem

Now, using these definitions, we can make the following claim.

> **Power of a Point.** Let $\omega$ be a circle and $P$ be a point on $\omega$. Points $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ lie on $\omega$ such that $AC$ and $BD$ intersect at $P$. Then
>
> $$PA \cdot PC = PB \cdot PD.$$

Note that this is true regardless of where $P$ is. Whether $P$ is outside or inside the circle, it doesn't matter. The proof here may seem familiar. 

First, we will angle chase in order to find similar triangles, an outlined closely followed in the previous lesson. In this example, $ABCD$ is a cyclic quadrilateral. 

* Suppose $P$ is inside the circle: As $\angle DAC = \angle DBC$ and $\angle ACB = \angle ADB$ (which follows from the fact that they subtend the same arc), then $\triangle PAD \sim \triangle PBC$. Hence, from similar triangles:

  <div align="center">

  $$\dfrac{PA}{PD} = \dfrac{PB}{PC} \implies PA \cdot PC = PB \cdot PD$$

  </div>

* Suppose $P$ is outside the circle: As

  <div align="center">
    $\angle PAB = 180^\circ - \angle CDB = \angle PDC$ <br>
    $\angle PBA = 180^\circ - \angle DCA = \angle PCD$
  </div>

  which follows from the fact that opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral sum to $180^\circ$, then $\triangle PAB \sim \triangle PDC$. Hence, from similar triangles:

  <div align="center">

  $$\dfrac{PA}{PB} = \dfrac{PD}{PC} \implies PA \cdot PC = PB \cdot PD$$

  </div>

Regardless, the desired result follows. Now, it is also important to look at a neat corollary to this which is considered what happens if $P$ is outside the circle. In the above scenario, we essentially assumed that two lines each intersected the circle at two points. However, there exists a scenario where one line could be tangent. In that scenario, what happens?

We can actually think of this scenario as exactly the same. As we move say line passing through $P$, $A$, and $C$ closer and closer to the edge of circle to obtain the desired tangency, then $PA$ and $PC$ approach the same value. Therefore, we end up with the following conclusion.

> **Power of a Point.** Point $P$ lies on circle $\omega$. Points $X$, $Y$, $Z$ lie on $\omega$ such that $XY$ passes through $P$ and $PZ$ is tangent to $\omega$. Then
>
> $$PX \cdot PY = PZ^2.$$

The proof isn't too far off from the proof for the case when $P$ is outside the circle mentioned above, so I won't go into too much detail. Rather, I will leave it as an exercise for the reader.

### The Converse Holds!

Although this may not shock you, it should. This is one of the key ideas in many, many Olympiad geometry problems. This, in particular, is one of the many ways to prove a quadrilateral is cyclic.

> **Converse of Power of a Point.** Let $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, and $P$ be points such that
> $$PA \cdot PC = PB \cdot PD.$$
> It follows that $ABCD$ is cyclic.

Nicely enough, the proof for this is almost the exact same as above but whose steps are taken in reverse. To stress once again, while the initial statement can heavily be used for finding lengths in computational contests, this converse proposal is a key idea in many, many Olympiad geometry problems which we will look at very soon.

## Putting everything together

> **Example 1** (American Mathematics Competition). In unit square $ABCD$, the inscribed circle $\omega$ intersects $CD$ at $M$, and $AM$ intersects $\omega$ at a point $P$ different from $M$. Compute $AP$.

The condition implies $M$ is the midpoint of $CD$. Due to Pythagoras,

$$AM = \sqrt{AD^2 + DM^2} = \sqrt{1^2+\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^2} = \tfrac{\sqrt{5}}{2}.$$

Let $N$ be the point of tangency between $\omega$ and $AD$. Then, by taking Power of the Point at $A$,

$$AN^2 = AM \cdot AP \implies \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \dfrac{\sqrt{5}}{2}\cdot AP \implies AP = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{5}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{5}}{10}.$$

> **Example 2** (USA Junior Mathematics Olympiad). Let $ABCD$ be a convex quadrilateral inscribed in a circle and satisfying $DA < AB = BC < CD$. Points $E$ and $F$ are chosen on sides $CD$ and $AB$ such that $BE \perp AC$ and $EF \parallel BC$. Prove that $FB = FD$.

Angle chasing, we have that

$$\angle FBD = \angle ABD = \angle ACD = \angle ACE.$$

In addition,

$$\angle FED + \angle FAD = BCD + \left(180^{\circ} - \angle BCD\right) = 180^{\circ},$$

thus $AFED$ is cyclic. So, $\angle AFD = \angle AED$, so $\angle BFD = \angle AEC$. Thus, $\triangle BFD \sim \triangle AEC$. Since $AE = EC$ as $E$ is on the perpendicular bisector of $AC$, then $FB = FD$, as desired.

## Alternate Form

Consider a circle $\omega$ with center $O$ and radius $r$. Let $P$ be an arbitrary point. Then, we define the power of $P$ with respect to $\omega$ to be

$$\text{Pow}(\omega, P) = PO^2 - r^2.$$

This will have more use later. But, for now, we will let allow a lingering question about what this function can do and its power.

A quick way to see the usefulness of this is the following: let $AB$ be a diameter of $\omega$ passing through $P$. Then, $PA \cdot PB = (PO-r)(PO+r)$. it follows that for any points $A$ and $B$ where segment $AB$ contains $P$, then $\text{Pow}(\omega, P) = AP \cdot PB$.
