## Overview

Angle bisectors convert information about angles into side ratios, most commonly using the Angle Bisector Theorem. They are especially useful in geometry problems involving ratios, areas, cevians, and triangle side lengths.


## Definitions and Key Ideas

A **cevian** is a line that connectes a vertex to a triangle's opposite side (or its extension). An angle bisector is an example of a cevian; medians and altitudes are as well.

An **angle bisector** is a line drawn from a vertex $A$ such that $\angle A$ is divided into two angles of equal measure. The bisector from a vertex lies inside the triangle and hits the opposite side between the endpoints.

The three angle bisectors intersect at the **incenter,** which is the center of the circle tangent to all three sides of a triangle.
 
- The angle bisector is equidistant from the sides of the angle it bisects. 
- Thus, the incenter is equidistant from all three sides (this distance is the **inradius**).
- For any **tangential** polygon, it holds that Area $K = r s,$ where $r$ is the inradius and $s$ is the **semiperimeter** (half the perimeter). Quadrilaterals are tangential iff pairs opposite sides have the same sum (Pitot's Theorem).
- The incenter's tangency points split the perimeter into 6 segments, 2 each of length $s-a, s-b, s-c.$
- (Angle Bisector Theorem) If $AD$ bisects $\angle A$ in triangle $ABC$, then
  $\frac{BD}{DC} = \frac{AB}{AC}$.
- Triangles $ABD$ and $ACD$ share a height from $A$, so their areas are in the
  ratio $BD:DC$.

## Properties

**Equidistance:** An angle bisector is equidistant from the sides of the angle it bisects. Let $AD$ bisect $\angle A$ in triangle $ABC$, and let $P$ be a point on the angle bisector. Drop altitudes $M$ and $N$ to $AB$ and $AC.$ Then by AAS congruence, $\triangle AMP \cong \triangle ANP,$ so $MP=NP$ and we are done.
The **converse** (that if a point is equidistant from two sides, it lies on the corresponding angle bisector) is also true, and can be proven using HL congruence.

**Incenter:** The three angle bisectors in a triangle can be proven to intersect at one point: the **incenter** $I.$ Due to the above property, the incenter must be equidistant from all three sides. Thus, we can draw a circle with **inradius** $MI=NI=LI,$ this is the **incircle,** and is tangent to all three sides of the triangle (recall that the radius to the point of tangency is perpendicular to the tangent line, and vice versa).

**Area:** For any polygon with an incircle (not just triangles!), their area is equal to $rs,$ inradius times semiperimeter. The proof uses simple area techniques. Connect the incenter to each of the vertices. This will divide the polygon into triangles. Then draw the inradii with length $r$; these are also the altitudes from the incenter to each of the sides, so we can use them as an altitude for each triangle as well. Using $\frac{1}{2}bh,$ the area of the polygon $ABCD(...)$ is 

$$\dfrac{1}{2} \left[ (AB)(r) + (BC)(r) + (CD) r + \cdots \right] $$

$$ = \dfrac{1}{2} (AB + BC + CD + \cdots) r$$

$$ = rs.$$

**Segments:** Because of the two tangents theorem, or alternatively triangle congruence, we know that $AM=AN, BM=BL, CN=CL.$ Call the respective quantities $x,y,$ and $z.$ If we are given $AB=c,BC=a,$ and $AC=b,$ we can set up the following system

$$x+y=c$$

$$y+z=a$$

$$z+x=b.$$

Solving and abbreviating $s=\frac{a+b+c}{2}$ (semiperimeter), it yields

$$x=s-a$$

$$y=s-b$$

$$z=s-c.$$


*Corollary:* All triangles have an incenter, but not all polygons with 4 (or more) sides do. Quadrilaterals that have an incircle are called **tangential.** By **Pitot's theorem,** a quadrilateral is tangential if and only if **the sums of the lengths of its *opposite* sides are equal.** This would allow us to split the quadrilateral into 8 segments (4 pairs of equal length from the same vertex). However, we generally cannot find the length of these segments directly from the sides of the quadrilateral as we did with the triangle equation system, since *quadrilaterals are not uniquely determined by their side lengths.*

 

### The Angle Bisector Theorem


If $AD$ bisects $\angle A$ in triangle $ABC$, then
  $\frac{BD}{DC} = \frac{AB}{AC}$.

### Proof

Drop altitudes from $D$ to $AB$ and $AC.$ As proved above, an angle bisector is equidistant from the sides of the angle it bisects. Thus, the altitudes have the same length, say $h.$ Thus, $[ABD] = \frac{1}{2} h AB,$ and $[ADC] = \frac{1}{2} h AC.$ Therefore,

$$\dfrac{[ABD]}{[ADC]} = \dfrac{AB}{AC}.$$

Next, notice that $\triangle ABD$ and $\triangle ADC$ share an altitude from $A$ to $BC.$ Thus, their areas satisfy

$$\dfrac{[ABD]}{[ADC]} = \dfrac{BD}{DC}.$$

Thus, we conclude that $\frac{AB}{AC} = \frac{BD}{DC}.$


### Applications

- Sometimes the lengths in the ratio statement will not be directly given to you. If the full side $BC$ is given, set a variable for $BD$ or $DC$ and use $BD+DC=BC$ and the ABT to solve for it.
- If the solution involves areas, note that because $ABD$ and $ACD$ share an altitude from $A$, their areas are proportional to $BD$ and $DC$.
- Look for tangential quadrilaterals using Pitot's theorem.

## Common Pitfalls

- Thinking that the equal ratios of two sides and angle makes $\triangle ABD$ and $\triangle ACD$ similar (they aren't; this is faulty SSA similarity) 
- Mixing up side lengths and segment lengths.
- If your diagram is close to equilateral, thinking a median or altitude is an angle bisector based on appearance.
- Reversing the ratio: $AB/AC = BD/DC,$ not $DC/BD.$
- Forgetting that the ratio uses the adjacent sides to the bisected angle. Watch out for this when there are multiple other lines going through the same point in addition to the angle bisector.
- Applying Pitot's when same sides are equal instead of opposite sides.

## Worked Example

In triangle $ABC$, $AB=8$, $AC=6$, and $BC=7$. The bisector from $A$ meets $BC$ at $D$. Find $BD$.

$$
\frac{BD}{DC} = \frac{8}{6} = \frac{4}{3}.
$$

Since

$$
BD+DC=7,
$$

the segments are $4$ and $3$, so

$$
BD=4.
$$

## More Examples

### Example 1: Quick Ratio

In triangle $ABC$, $AB=5$ and $AC=10$. The angle bisector from $A$ meets $BC$ at $D$. Find $BD:DC$.

By the Angle Bisector Theorem,

$$
BD:DC = AB:AC = 1:2.
$$

### Example 2: Area Ratio

If

$$ 
[ABD]=12\qquad\text{and}\qquad[ACD]=18,
$$

find $BD:DC$.

The triangles share an altitude from $A$, so

$$ 
BD:DC = 12:18 = 2:3.
$$

### Example 3: Finding Segments

In triangle $ABC$, $AB=12$, $AC=18$, and $BC=20$. The angle bisector from $A$ meets $BC$ at $D$. Find $DC$.

The Angle Bisector Theorem gives

$$
BD:DC = 12:18 = 2:3.
$$

Since

$$
BD+DC=20,
$$

the side is divided into $5$ equal parts.

Each part has length $4$, so

$$
DC=12.
$$

### Example 4: Area From Side Ratios

In triangle $DIH$, the angle bisector from $D$ meets $IH$ at $C$. Suppose

$$
DI=9,\qquad DH=6,
$$

and

$$
[DIH]=30.
$$

Find $[DIC]$.

Since

$$
DI:DH=9:6=3:2,
$$

we have

$$
[DIC]:[DHC]=3:2.
$$

The total area is $30$, so

$$
[DIC] = \frac{3}{5}\cdot30 = 18.
$$
