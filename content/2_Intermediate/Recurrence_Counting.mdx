## Overview

A recurrence relation defines a sequence using earlier terms.

In counting problems, recurrences are useful when a large object can be built
from smaller objects.

For example, the Fibonacci numbers satisfy

$$
F_n=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}.
$$

This type of recurrence appears naturally in tiling problems, stair climbing
problems, and arrangements with restrictions.

The key idea is:

> Count the final object by looking at how it could have ended.

## Motivation

Suppose we want to tile a board of length $n$ using:
- Squares of length $1$,
- Dominoes of length $2$.

Let

$$
T_n
$$

be the number of ways to tile a board of length $n$.

Look at the last tile.

If the last tile is a square, then the first $n-1$ positions can be tiled in

$$
T_{n-1}
$$

ways.

If the last tile is a domino, then the first $n-2$ positions can be tiled in

$$
T_{n-2}
$$

ways.

Therefore,

$$
T_n=T_{n-1}+T_{n-2}.
$$

This is the Fibonacci recurrence.

## Base Cases

A recurrence is incomplete without base cases.

For the tiling example:

$$
T_0=1
$$

and

$$
T_1=1.
$$

The value

$$
T_0=1
$$

means there is exactly one way to tile an empty board, use no tiles.

This convention often makes recurrence formulas work cleanly.

Using

$$
T_n=T_{n-1}+T_{n-2},
$$

we get

$$
T_2=2,
$$

$$
T_3=3,
$$

$$
T_4=5,
$$

and so on.

## Building a Recurrence

To build a recurrence, ask:
1. What quantity should the sequence count?
2. What is the last step or final piece?
3. What smaller problem remains after removing it?
4. What are the base cases?

The hardest part is choosing the right cases so that they do not overlap and do
not miss anything.

## Tiling With Squares and Dominoes

### Example 1

> How many ways are there to tile a $1\times 7$ board using $1\times 1$
> squares and $1\times 2$ dominoes?

Let

$$
T_n
$$

be the number of tilings of a $1\times n$ board.

As above,

$$
T_n=T_{n-1}+T_{n-2}.
$$

The base cases are

$$
T_0=1,
\qquad
T_1=1.
$$

Now compute:

$$
T_2=2,
$$

$$
T_3=3,
$$

$$
T_4=5,
$$

$$
T_5=8,
$$

$$
T_6=13,
$$

$$
T_7=21.
$$

So there are

$$
21
$$

tilings.

## Stair-Climbing Problems

Recurrences also appear in stair problems.

Suppose a person can climb either $1$ step or $2$ steps at a time.

Let

$$
S_n
$$

be the number of ways to climb $n$ steps.

The final move is either:
- A $1$-step move from step $n-1$
- A $2$-step move from step $n-2$

So

$$
S_n=S_{n-1}+S_{n-2}.
$$

Again, the Fibonacci recurrence appears.

## Tiling With More Tile Sizes

If more tile sizes are allowed, the recurrence changes.

### Example 2

> How many ways are there to tile a $1\times n$ board using tiles of lengths
> $1$, $2$, and $3$?

Let

$$
T_n
$$

be the number of tilings.

The last tile can have length $1$, $2$, or $3$.

Therefore,

$$
T_n=T_{n-1}+T_{n-2}+T_{n-3}.
$$

The base cases are

$$
T_0=1,
$$

$$
T_1=1,
$$

and

$$
T_2=2.
$$

## Avoiding Consecutive Objects

Recurrences can count arrangements with restrictions.

### Example 3

> How many binary strings of length $n$ have no two consecutive $1$'s?

Let

$$
A_n
$$

be the number of valid strings of length $n$.

Look at the end of the string.

If the string ends in $0$, then the first $n-1$ digits can be any valid string.
This gives

$$
A_{n-1}
$$

strings.

If the string ends in $1$, then the previous digit must be $0$. So the string
ends in

$$
01.
$$

The first $n-2$ digits can be any valid string. This gives

$$
A_{n-2}
$$

strings.

Therefore,

$$
A_n=A_{n-1}+A_{n-2}.
$$

The base cases are

$$
A_1=2
$$

and

$$
A_2=3.
$$

## Recurrences With Several States

Sometimes one sequence is not enough.

If a problem has different ending types, it may be useful to define multiple
states.

### Example 4

> How many length-$n$ strings made from $A$ and $B$ have no three consecutive
> $A$'s?

Let

$$
x_n
$$

be the number of valid strings of length $n$ ending in $B$.

Let

$$
y_n
$$

be the number ending in exactly one $A$.

Let

$$
z_n
$$

be the number ending in exactly two consecutive $A$'s.

Then

$$
x_{n+1}=x_n+y_n+z_n,
$$

because we may add $B$ to any valid string.

Also,

$$
y_{n+1}=x_n,
$$

because to end in exactly one $A$, the previous character must be $B$.

Finally,

$$
z_{n+1}=y_n,
$$

because to end in exactly two consecutive $A$'s, the previous string must have
ended in exactly one $A$.

The total number of valid strings is

$$
x_n+y_n+z_n.
$$

This state method is often cleaner than trying to force one recurrence too
early.

## Solving Simple Linear Recurrences

Some recurrences can be solved exactly.

For example,

$$
F_n=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}
$$

has a closed form, but in most contest counting problems we only need to compute
terms up to a certain value.

For recurrences like

$$
a_n=2a_{n-1},
$$

the solution is exponential.

If

$$
a_0=1,
$$

then

$$
a_n=2^n.
$$

If

$$
a_n=a_{n-1}+3
$$

with

$$
a_0=2,
$$

then

$$
a_n=2+3n.
$$

The form of the recurrence reflects the growth of the counting process.

## Worked Examples

### Example 5

> A rabbit can jump either $1$, $2$, or $3$ spaces at a time. How many ways can
> it reach space $6$?

Let

$$
R_n
$$

be the number of ways to reach space $n$.

The last jump could have size $1$, $2$, or $3$, so

$$
R_n=R_{n-1}+R_{n-2}+R_{n-3}.
$$

Use base cases:

$$
R_0=1,
\qquad
R_1=1,
\qquad
R_2=2.
$$

Then

$$
R_3=R_2+R_1+R_0=4,
$$

$$
R_4=R_3+R_2+R_1=7,
$$

$$
R_5=R_4+R_3+R_2=13,
$$

$$
R_6=R_5+R_4+R_3=24.
$$

So there are

$$
24
$$

ways.

### Example 6

> How many ways are there to tile a $1\times 8$ board using only dominoes of
> length $2$ and trominoes of length $3$?

Let

$$
T_n
$$

be the number of tilings of length $n$.

The last tile has length $2$ or $3$, so

$$
T_n=T_{n-2}+T_{n-3}.
$$

We use

$$
T_0=1,
$$

$$
T_1=0,
$$

and

$$
T_2=1.
$$

Then

$$
T_3=1,
$$

$$
T_4=T_2+T_1=1,
$$

$$
T_5=T_3+T_2=2,
$$

$$
T_6=T_4+T_3=2,
$$

$$
T_7=T_5+T_4=3,
$$

$$
T_8=T_6+T_5=4.
$$

So there are

$$
4
$$

tilings.

## Key Contest Insight

Recurrence problems are usually solved by looking at the last step.

If the final choice leaves a smaller version of the same problem, a recurrence
is likely useful.

The most important part is choosing cases that are:
- Complete
- Non-overlapping
- Easy to translate into smaller subproblems

## Strategy Tips
- Define the sequence clearly
- Decide what $a_n$ counts
- Look at the last tile, last step, or last symbol
- Write the recurrence from smaller cases
- Choose base cases carefully
- Use $a_0=1$ when counting empty constructions
- If one sequence is not enough, introduce multiple states
- Compute terms patiently and check small cases

## Common Pitfalls
- Forgetting base cases
- Using the wrong value for the empty case
- Double-counting overlapping cases
- Missing a possible final step
- Defining $a_n$ vaguely
- Trying to solve a recurrence before setting it up correctly
