## Overview

Polynomial division is the polynomial version of ordinary division.

When a polynomial $P(x)$ is divided by another polynomial $D(x)$, we can write

$$
P(x)=D(x)Q(x)+R(x),
$$

where:
- $Q(x)$ is the quotient
- $R(x)$ is the remainder
- The degree of $R(x)$ is less than the degree of $D(x)$

This is called the division algorithm for polynomials.

The most important cases in contest algebra are:
- Dividing by a linear polynomial
- Using the Remainder Theorem
- Using the Factor Theorem
- Finding remainders when dividing by a quadratic

## Polynomial Division

Suppose we divide

$$
P(x)
$$

by

$$
D(x).
$$

Then

$$
P(x)=D(x)Q(x)+R(x),
$$

where

$$
\deg R<\deg D.
$$

For example, if $D(x)$ is linear, then $R(x)$ must be constant.

If $D(x)$ is quadratic, then $R(x)$ has degree at most $1$, so it has the form

$$
ax+b.
$$

This degree condition is the key to many remainder problems.

## Dividing by a Linear Polynomial

When dividing by

$$
x-a,
$$

the remainder is a constant.

So we may write

$$
P(x)=(x-a)Q(x)+r.
$$

Now substitute

$$
x=a.
$$

Since

$$
a-a=0,
$$

we get

$$
P(a)=r.
$$

Therefore, the remainder when $P(x)$ is divided by $x-a$ is

$$
P(a).
$$

This is the Remainder Theorem.

## Remainder Theorem

If a polynomial $P(x)$ is divided by

$$
x-a,
$$

then the remainder is

$$
P(a).
$$

### Example 1

> Find the remainder when
>
> $$
> P(x)=x^4+3x^2-5x+7
> $$
>
> is divided by $x-2$.

By the Remainder Theorem, the remainder is

$$
P(2).
$$

Compute:

$$
P(2)=2^4+3(2^2)-5(2)+7.
$$

So

$$
P(2)=16+12-10+7=25.
$$

The remainder is

$$
25.
$$

## Factor Theorem

The Factor Theorem says:

$$
x-a
$$

is a factor of $P(x)$ if and only if

$$
P(a)=0.
$$

This follows directly from the Remainder Theorem.

If the remainder when dividing by $x-a$ is $0$, then $x-a$ divides $P(x)$.

### Example 2

> Show that $x-3$ is a factor of
>
> $$
> P(x)=x^3-4x^2+x+6.
> $$

Use the Factor Theorem.

Compute:

$$
P(3)=3^3-4(3^2)+3+6.
$$

Thus

$$
P(3)=27-36+3+6=0.
$$

Therefore,

$$
x-3
$$

is a factor of $P(x)$.

## Remainder by a Quadratic

If a polynomial $P(x)$ is divided by a quadratic polynomial, the remainder must
have degree less than $2$.

So the remainder has the form

$$
R(x)=ax+b.
$$

So,

$$
P(x)=D(x)Q(x)+ax+b.
$$

To find $a$ and $b$, we use values of $x$ that make $D(x)=0$.

## Quadratic With Distinct Linear Factors

Suppose we divide by

$$
(x-r)(x-s),
$$

where

$$
r\neq s.
$$

Then the remainder has the form

$$
R(x)=ax+b.
$$

Since

$$
P(x)=(x-r)(x-s)Q(x)+R(x),
$$

substitute $x=r$:

$$
P(r)=R(r).
$$

Substitute $x=s$:

$$
P(s)=R(s).
$$

So we solve

$$
ar+b=P(r)
$$

and

$$
as+b=P(s).
$$

This gives the remainder.

## Worked Quadratic Example

### Example 3

> Find the remainder when
>
> $$
> P(x)=x^5+2x^3+1
> $$
>
> is divided by
>
> $$
> x^2-1.
> $$

Since

$$
x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1),
$$

the remainder has the form

$$
R(x)=ax+b.
$$

Now use the roots $1$ and $-1$.

First,

$$
P(1)=1^5+2(1^3)+1=4.
$$

So

$$
R(1)=a+b=4.
$$

Next,

$$
P(-1)=(-1)^5+2(-1)^3+1=-1-2+1=-2.
$$

So

$$
R(-1)=-a+b=-2.
$$

Solving

$$
a+b=4
$$

and

$$
-a+b=-2,
$$

we get

$$
a=3
$$

and

$$
b=1.
$$

Therefore, the remainder is

$$
3x+1.
$$

## Remainder by an Irreducible Quadratic

Sometimes the quadratic does not factor nicely over the real numbers.

For example, suppose we divide by

$$
x^2+1.
$$

Since

$$
x^2+1=0
$$

implies

$$
x^2=-1,
$$

we can reduce powers of $x$ using

$$
x^2\equiv -1 \pmod{x^2+1}.
$$

This means we simplify the polynomial while replacing every $x^2$ by $-1$.

### Example 4

> Find the remainder when
>
> $$
> x^6+2x^5+x^2+3
> $$
>
> is divided by
>
> $$
> x^2+1.
> $$

Modulo $x^2+1$, we have

$$
x^2\equiv -1.
$$

Then

$$
x^4\equiv 1
$$

and

$$
x^6\equiv -1.
$$

Also,

$$
x^5=x(x^4)\equiv x.
$$

Therefore,

$$
x^6+2x^5+x^2+3
\equiv
-1+2x-1+3.
$$

So the remainder is

$$
2x+1.
$$

## Remainder by a Repeated Linear Factor

If the divisor is

$$
(x-a)^2,
$$

then the remainder has the form

$$
R(x)=mx+b.
$$

We need two conditions.

Since

$$
P(x)=(x-a)^2Q(x)+R(x),
$$

substitute $x=a$:

$$
P(a)=R(a).
$$

Differentiate both sides:

$$
P'(x)=2(x-a)Q(x)+(x-a)^2Q'(x)+R'(x).
$$

Now substitute $x=a$:

$$
P'(a)=R'(a).
$$

So for division by $(x-a)^2$, use

$$
R(a)=P(a)
$$

and

$$
R'(a)=P'(a).
$$

### Example 5

> Find the remainder when
>
> $$
> P(x)=x^4+x^2+1
> $$
>
> is divided by
>
> $$
> (x-1)^2.
> $$

The remainder has the form

$$
R(x)=mx+b.
$$

First,

$$
P(1)=1+1+1=3.
$$

So

$$
R(1)=m+b=3.
$$

Now

$$
P'(x)=4x^3+2x.
$$

Thus

$$
P'(1)=4+2=6.
$$

Since

$$
R'(x)=m,
$$

we get

$$
m=6.
$$

Then

$$
6+b=3,
$$

so

$$
b=-3.
$$

Therefore, the remainder is

$$
6x-3.
$$

## Polynomial Congruences

It is often useful to think of remainders using polynomial congruences.

Writing

$$
P(x)\equiv R(x)\pmod{D(x)}
$$

means that $P(x)$ and $R(x)$ have the same remainder when divided by $D(x)$.

For example,

$$
x^2\equiv 1 \pmod{x^2-1}.
$$

So when working modulo $x^2-1$, we may replace $x^2$ with $1$.

This is similar to ordinary modular arithmetic, but with polynomials.

## Worked Examples

### Example 6

> Find the remainder when
>
> $$
> x^{2026}
> $$
>
> is divided by
>
> $$
> x^2-1.
> $$

Modulo $x^2-1$, we have

$$
x^2\equiv 1.
$$

Therefore,

$$
x^{2026}=(x^2)^{1013}\equiv 1^{1013}\equiv 1.
$$

So the remainder is

$$
1.
$$

### Example 7

> Find the remainder when
>
> $$
> x^{2025}
> $$
>
> is divided by
>
> $$
> x^2-1.
> $$

Again,

$$
x^2\equiv 1 \pmod{x^2-1}.
$$

Then

$$
x^{2025}=x(x^2)^{1012}\equiv x.
$$

So the remainder is

$$
x.
$$

## Key Contest Insight

The remainder always has smaller degree than the divisor.

This is the main idea.

- When dividing by $x-a$, the remainder is constant
- When dividing by a quadratic, the remainder has the form $ax+b$
- When dividing by $(x-a)^2$, use both the value and derivative at $a$

For large powers, reduce using the relation created by the divisor.

## Strategy Tips

- Identify the degree of the divisor
- Write the most general possible remainder
- For divisor $x-a$, use $P(a)$
- For divisor $(x-r)(x-s)$, use the two roots
- For divisor $(x-a)^2$, use $P(a)$ and $P'(a)$
- For divisors like $x^2+1$, reduce powers using the relation $x^2=-1$
- Always check that the remainder has lower degree than the divisor

## Common Pitfalls

- Forgetting that the remainder by a quadratic is linear or constant
- Using only one value when two conditions are needed
- Forgetting the derivative condition for repeated roots
- Making sign errors when substituting negative roots
- Leaving a remainder with degree too large
- Confusing numerical modular arithmetic with polynomial congruences
