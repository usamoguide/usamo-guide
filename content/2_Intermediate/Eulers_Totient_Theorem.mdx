## Introduction
In this unit we will discuss and prove **Euler's Totient Theorem**, which states that if $n$ and $a$ are coprime positive integers, then

$$
a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod{n}
$$

Here, $\varphi(n)$ denotes Euler's totient function, which counts the number of positive integers smaller than $n$ which are coprime to $n$.

The converse of the theorem is also true; if the congruence holds, then $a$ and $n$ are coprime.

The special case where $n$ is prime is known as **Fermat's Little Theorem**, which states that if $p$ is prime and $a$ is an integer not divisible by $p$, then

$$
a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}
$$

## Proof of Euler's Totient Theorem
Now we can return to our original task. Let $a,n$ be coprime positive integers. Define the set $R$ to be all positive integers smaller than $n$ that are coprime to $n$. This set of course has $\varphi(n)$ elements, which we will label $R=\{x_1, x_2, ..., x_{\varphi(n)}\}$.

Our proof hinges on the fact that multiplying all the $x_i$ by $a$ will permute them mod $n$, or in other words:

*Claim:* Let $aR = \{a\cdot x_1, a\cdot x_2, ..., a\cdot x_{\varphi(n)} \}$. Then $aR$ is identical to $R$ when considered mod $n$. (they are sets so they may be ordered differently)

*Proof:* Every element of $aR$ is coprime to $n$, and is thus congruent to some element of $R$. Thus we only need to prove that no two distinct elements of $aR$ can be congruent.

Assume we have $i,j$ such that $a\cdot x_i \equiv a\cdot x_j \pmod{n}$. Then we can multply both sides of the congruence by the modular inverse of $a$ to get $x_i \equiv x_j \pmod{n}$, concluding the proof. $\square$

Since $R$ and $aR$ are identical mod $n$, the products of all the elements of $R$ is congruent to the product of all the elements of $aR$.

$$
\prod_{i=1}^{\varphi(n)} x_i \equiv 
\prod_{i=1}^{\varphi(n)} ax_i =
a^{\varphi(n)}\prod_{i=1}^{\varphi(n)} x_i \pmod{n}
$$

We can then multiply both sides by the modular inverse of each $x_i$ to get

$$
a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv 1 \pmod{n}. 
$$

## Applications
Euler's Totient Theorem is mainly used in number theory problems involving exponents. In particular, it tells us that $a^{b} \equiv a^{b \pmod{\varphi(n)}} \pmod{n}$ when $a,n$ coprime. Here is a direct application of this fact:

> Let $a_n=6^{n}+8^{n}$. Determine the remainder upon dividing $a_ {83}$ by $49$. (1983 AIME #6)

*Solution:* $\varphi(49)=42$. Thus $6^{83} + 8^{83} \equiv 6^{-1} + 8^{-1} \equiv \frac{8+6}{48} \equiv \frac{14}{-1} \equiv \boxed{035} \pmod{49}$.
