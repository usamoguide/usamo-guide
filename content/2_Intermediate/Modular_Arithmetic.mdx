## Overview

Modular arithmetic is arithmetic with remainders. Instead of caring about the exact value of a huge integer, we often care only about what remainder it leaves after division by some number.

For example, $17$ and $5$ are different integers, but they both leave remainder $5$ when divided by $12$. We write
$$17\equiv5\pmod{12}.$$

This simple idea is extremely powerful. Modular arithmetic can turn enormous powers, complicated divisibility questions, and impossible-looking integer equations into short computations.

It is especially useful for:
- remainders and last digits
- divisibility
- parity
- powers and repeating cycles
- proving integer equations impossible
- narrowing down possible values in Diophantine equations

## Motivation

Suppose we want the last digit of
$$7^{2026}.$$

The last digit only depends on the remainder modulo $10$, so compute powers of $7$ modulo $10$:
$$7^1\equiv7\pmod{10}$$
$$7^2\equiv9\pmod{10}$$
$$7^3\equiv3\pmod{10}$$
$$7^4\equiv1\pmod{10}.$$

Now the pattern repeats every $4$ powers:
$$7,9,3,1,7,9,3,1,\ldots$$

Since
$$2026\equiv2\pmod4,$$

$7^{2026}$ has the same final digit as $7^2$. Therefore the last digit is
$$\boxed9.$$

The point is not just that modular arithmetic finds the answer. It lets us completely ignore the enormous size of $7^{2026}$.

## Congruences

### Definition

For a positive integer $m$, we write
$$a\equiv b\pmod m$$
if $a$ and $b$ leave the same remainder when divided by $m$.

Equivalently,
$$m\mid(a-b).$$

So saying
$$a\equiv b\pmod m$$
means that $a-b$ is a multiple of $m$.

### Examples

Since
$$17-5=12,$$
we have
$$17\equiv5\pmod{12}.$$

Since
$$38-2=36,$$
we have
$$38\equiv2\pmod9.$$

Negative residues work too. Since
$$-3-4=-7,$$
we have
$$-3\equiv4\pmod7.$$

## Thinking in Residue Classes

Modulo $m$, every integer is equivalent to one of
$$0,1,2,\ldots,m-1.$$

For example, modulo $5$,
$$12\equiv2,\qquad27\equiv2,\qquad102\equiv2\pmod5.$$

All three numbers behave the same way modulo $5$.

A useful mental model is a clock. Modulo $12$, after reaching $11$ we wrap back around to $0$.

For example,
$$10+5=15\equiv3\pmod{12}.$$

## Basic Operations

If
$$a\equiv b\pmod m$$
and
$$c\equiv d\pmod m,$$
then we may add:
$$a+c\equiv b+d\pmod m.$$

We may subtract:
$$a-c\equiv b-d\pmod m.$$

We may multiply:
$$ac\equiv bd\pmod m.$$

We may also take positive integer powers:
$$a^k\equiv b^k\pmod m.$$

These rules mean that we can replace numbers by simpler representatives whenever we want.

### Example

Find
$$47^2+32\pmod5.$$

Reduce first:
$$47\equiv2\pmod5$$
and
$$32\equiv2\pmod5.$$

Therefore,
$$47^2+32\equiv2^2+2=6\equiv1\pmod5.$$

So the remainder is
$$\boxed1.$$

## Reduce Early and Often

Do not carry around unnecessarily large numbers.

For example, find
$$38^4\pmod7.$$

First,
$$38\equiv3\pmod7.$$

Thus,
$$38^4\equiv3^4=81\pmod7.$$

Now,
$$81\equiv4\pmod7.$$

Therefore,
$$\boxed4.$$

The calculation $38^4$ was never needed.

## Negative Residues

The standard remainder is not always the easiest number to use.

For example,
$$19\equiv-1\pmod{10}.$$

Therefore,
$$19^{101}\equiv(-1)^{101}\equiv-1\equiv9\pmod{10}.$$

Using $-1$ is much easier than repeatedly working with $9$.

Similarly,
$$48\equiv-2\pmod{10}$$
and
$$99\equiv-1\pmod{100}.$$

A good habit is to choose the residue with the smallest absolute value.

## Important Warning: Division

Addition, subtraction, and multiplication work naturally modulo $m$. Division is different.

For example,
$$2\cdot1\equiv2\cdot4\pmod6$$
because both sides are congruent to $2$ modulo $6$.

But
$$1\not\equiv4\pmod6.$$

So cancelling the $2$ was illegal.

### Cancellation Rule

If
$$ac\equiv bc\pmod m$$
and
$$\gcd(c,m)=1,$$
then we may cancel $c$:
$$a\equiv b\pmod m.$$

The coprimality condition is essential.

## Modular Inverses

Instead of thinking of modular division as ordinary division, think of it as multiplication by an inverse.

An inverse of $a$ modulo $m$ is a number $b$ such that
$$ab\equiv1\pmod m.$$

An inverse exists exactly when
$$\gcd(a,m)=1.$$

### Example

Find the inverse of $3$ modulo $7$.

We want
$$3b\equiv1\pmod7.$$

Since
$$3\cdot5=15\equiv1\pmod7,$$
the inverse is
$$3^{-1}\equiv5\pmod7.$$

## Solving Linear Congruences

Solve
$$3x\equiv5\pmod7.$$

The inverse of $3$ modulo $7$ is $5$, so multiply both sides by $5$:
$$x\equiv25\pmod7.$$

Reduce:
$$x\equiv4\pmod7.$$

Therefore,
$$\boxed{x\equiv4\pmod7}.$$

## Powers and Cycles

Powers modulo $m$ often repeat.

For example, powers of $2$ modulo $10$ are
$$2,4,8,6,2,4,8,6,\ldots$$

The cycle length is $4$.

To find the last digit of
$$2^{100},$$
compute
$$100\equiv0\pmod4.$$

So we use the fourth entry in the cycle:
$$\boxed6.$$

When looking for a cycle:
1. Reduce each power modulo $m$.
2. Stop when the residues begin repeating.
3. Reduce the exponent modulo the cycle length.

## Fermat's Little Theorem

Cycles can sometimes be found without listing powers.

If $p$ is prime and $p\nmid a$, then
$$a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p.$$

This is Fermat's Little Theorem.

### Example

Find
$$3^{2026}\pmod7.$$

Since $7$ is prime and $7\nmid3$,
$$3^6\equiv1\pmod7.$$

Now,
$$2026=6(337)+4.$$

Therefore,
$$3^{2026}\equiv3^4\pmod7.$$

Compute:
$$3^2=9\equiv2\pmod7.$$

Thus,
$$3^4\equiv2^2=4\pmod7.$$

So the remainder is
$$\boxed4.$$

## Choosing the Right Modulus

In many AIME problems, the hardest part is not doing modular arithmetic. It is deciding which modulus to use.

Here are some common choices:

- Use modulo $2$ for parity.
- Use modulo $4$ or $8$ for squares and parity.
- Use modulo $3$ or $9$ for digit sums.
- Use modulo $5$ or $10$ for last digits.
- Use modulo $11$ for alternating digit sums.
- Use modulo $100$ for last two digits.
- Use a prime modulus when Fermat's Little Theorem may help.
- Use a modulus that makes one side of an equation especially restricted.

The best modulus is usually the one that gives the fewest possible residues.

## Useful Residue Patterns

### Squares Modulo $4$

Every integer is congruent to $0,1,2,$ or $3$ modulo $4$.

Squaring:
$$0^2\equiv0\pmod4$$
$$1^2\equiv1\pmod4$$
$$2^2\equiv0\pmod4$$
$$3^2\equiv1\pmod4.$$

Therefore,
$$\boxed{x^2\equiv0\text{ or }1\pmod4}.$$

This is one of the most useful facts in elementary number theory.

### Odd Squares Modulo $8$

Every odd integer is congruent to $1,3,5,$ or $7$ modulo $8$.

Squaring any of these gives
$$x^2\equiv1\pmod8.$$

Therefore every odd square satisfies
$$\boxed{x^2\equiv1\pmod8}.$$

### Squares Modulo $3$

Checking $0,1,2$ gives
$$x^2\equiv0\text{ or }1\pmod3.$$

These small residue sets are very useful for proving that equations are impossible.

## Worked Example: Proving Impossibility

Show that no integers $x,y$ satisfy
$$x^2+y^2=4k+3$$
for any integer $k$.

Every square is congruent to $0$ or $1$ modulo $4$.

Therefore,
$$x^2+y^2\equiv0,1,\text{ or }2\pmod4.$$

But
$$4k+3\equiv3\pmod4.$$

The two sides cannot have the same remainder modulo $4$.

Therefore no integer solutions exist.

The important idea is that we did not try to solve the equation. We found a modulus under which the equation becomes impossible.

## Divisibility Tests

Many familiar divisibility rules are consequences of modular arithmetic.

Let
$$N=\overline{a_1a_2\cdots a_n}.$$

Then
$$N=a_1 10^{n-1}+a_2 10^{n-2}+\cdots+a_n.$$

### Divisibility by $3$

Since
$$10\equiv1\pmod3,$$
we have
$$10^k\equiv1\pmod3.$$

Therefore,
$$N\equiv a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n\pmod3.$$

So a number is divisible by $3$ exactly when its digit sum is divisible by $3$.

### Divisibility by $9$

Similarly,
$$10\equiv1\pmod9,$$
so the same digit-sum rule works modulo $9$.

### Divisibility by $11$

Since
$$10\equiv-1\pmod{11},$$
the powers of $10$ alternate:
$$1,-1,1,-1,\ldots$$

Therefore,
$$N\equiv a_n-a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}-a_{n-3}+\cdots\pmod{11}.$$

Thus a number is divisible by $11$ when its alternating digit sum is divisible by $11$.

## More Examples

### Example 1: Last Two Digits

Find the last two digits of
$$7^{222}.$$

We work modulo $100$.

Notice that
$$7^4=2401\equiv1\pmod{100}.$$

Since
$$222=4(55)+2,$$
we get
$$7^{222}=(7^4)^{55}7^2.$$

Therefore,
$$7^{222}\equiv1^{55}\cdot49\equiv49\pmod{100}.$$

So the last two digits are
$$\boxed{49}.$$

### Example 2: An Impossible Equation

Determine whether integers $x,y$ can satisfy
$$x^2+y^2=2027.$$

Reduce modulo $4$:
$$2027\equiv3\pmod4.$$

But
$$x^2+y^2\equiv0,1,\text{ or }2\pmod4.$$

Therefore the equation has no integer solutions.

### Example 3: Using a Clever Modulus

Suppose
$$x^2=3y+2$$
for integers $x,y$.

Reducing modulo $3$ gives
$$x^2\equiv2\pmod3.$$

But squares modulo $3$ are only $0$ and $1$.

Therefore no integer solutions exist.

This illustrates an important strategy: if one side contains a multiple of $3$ plus a constant, modulo $3$ is a natural first choice.

### Example 4: Large Exponent

Find
$$13^{100}\pmod5.$$

First,
$$13\equiv3\pmod5.$$

Then,
$$3^2=9\equiv-1\pmod5.$$

Therefore,
$$3^{100}=(3^2)^{50}\equiv(-1)^{50}=1\pmod5.$$

Hence,
$$\boxed{13^{100}\equiv1\pmod5}.$$

## Key Olympiad Strategy

When you see an integer problem, ask:

1. What happens if I reduce everything modulo a small number?
2. Are the possible residues of squares, cubes, or powers restricted?
3. Can I choose a modulus that makes one side become $0$, $1$, or $-1$?
4. Does the problem involve last digits or divisibility?
5. Do powers repeat in a short cycle?
6. Can a congruence eliminate most or all possible cases?

Modular arithmetic is often strongest when used to eliminate possibilities rather than directly construct the answer.

## Common Pitfalls

- Congruence is not ordinary equality.
- Do not cancel a factor unless it is coprime to the modulus.
- Reduce numbers before doing large computations.
- Do not assume a power cycle continues without identifying why it repeats.
- Remember that a negative residue such as $-1$ may be easier than the standard positive residue.
- Do not choose a modulus randomly. Choose one that interacts with the structure of the problem.
- When checking possible square or cube residues, make sure you test every residue class modulo that modulus.

## Strategy Checklist

- Reduce large numbers immediately.
- Try small moduli such as $2,3,4,5,8,9,10,$ and $11$.
- Use negative residues when convenient.
- Look for cycles in powers.
- Know common square residues.
- Use Fermat's Little Theorem for large powers modulo a prime.
- Check whether cancellation is actually legal.
- When proving impossibility, compare the possible residues of both sides.
- When solving a congruence, look for a modular inverse.
- Always ask why your chosen modulus is useful.
