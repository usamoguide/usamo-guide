## Overview

The order of an element modulo $n$ is a way to measure how quickly powers repeat
back to $1$.

For example, modulo $7$,

$$
3^1\equiv 3,
$$

$$
3^2\equiv 2,
$$

$$
3^3\equiv 6,
$$

$$
3^4\equiv 4,
$$

$$
3^5\equiv 5,
$$

$$
3^6\equiv 1.
$$

So the powers of $3$ first return to $1$ at exponent $6$.

We say that the order of $3$ modulo $7$ is $6$.

## Definition

Let $a$ and $n$ be positive integers with

$$
\gcd(a,n)=1.
$$

The order of $a$ modulo $n$, written

$$
\operatorname{ord}_n(a),
$$

is the smallest positive integer $k$ such that

$$
a^k\equiv 1 \pmod n.
$$

The condition

$$
\gcd(a,n)=1
$$

is important. If $a$ is not relatively prime to $n$, then the powers of $a$ may
never become congruent to $1$ modulo $n$.

## Motivation

Suppose we want to find the remainder of

$$
2^{100}
$$

modulo $7$.

Instead of computing the power directly, we look for a cycle.

Modulo $7$,

$$
2^1\equiv 2,
$$

$$
2^2\equiv 4,
$$

$$
2^3\equiv 8\equiv 1.
$$

So

$$
\operatorname{ord}_7(2)=3.
$$

This means powers of $2$ repeat every $3$ exponents modulo $7$.

Since

$$
100\equiv 1 \pmod 3,
$$

we get

$$
2^{100}\equiv 2^1\equiv 2 \pmod 7.
$$

The order turns a large exponent into a small one.

## Basic Properties

If

$$
\operatorname{ord}_n(a)=d,
$$

then

$$
a^d\equiv 1 \pmod n.
$$

Also,

$$
a^m\equiv 1 \pmod n
$$

if and only if

$$
d\mid m.
$$

This is one of the most useful facts about order.

### Why This Is True

Suppose

$$
m=qd+r,
$$

where

$$
0\le r<d.
$$

Then

$$
a^m=a^{qd+r}=(a^d)^q a^r.
$$

Since

$$
a^d\equiv 1 \pmod n,
$$

we get

$$
a^m\equiv a^r \pmod n.
$$

If

$$
a^m\equiv 1 \pmod n,
$$

then

$$
a^r\equiv 1 \pmod n.
$$

But $d$ was the smallest positive exponent giving $1$. Therefore,

$$
r=0.
$$

So

$$
d\mid m.
$$

## Connection to Euler's Theorem

Euler's Theorem says that if

$$
\gcd(a,n)=1,
$$

then

$$
a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv 1 \pmod n.
$$

Therefore, the order of $a$ modulo $n$ must divide

$$
\varphi(n).
$$

So

$$
\operatorname{ord}_n(a)\mid \varphi(n).
$$

This gives a powerful way to find the order: instead of testing every exponent,
we only test divisors of $\varphi(n)$.

For a prime $p$, we have

$$
\varphi(p)=p-1.
$$

So if $p$ is prime and $p\nmid a$, then

$$
\operatorname{ord}_p(a)\mid p-1.
$$

## Finding the Order

To find

$$
\operatorname{ord}_n(a),
$$

use the following method:

1. Check that $\gcd(a,n)=1$.
2. Compute $\varphi(n)$, if useful.
3. List possible divisors of $\varphi(n)$.
4. Test powers of $a$ until the first one congruent to $1$ appears.

The smallest valid exponent is the order.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1

> Find $\operatorname{ord}_7(3)$.

Compute powers of $3$ modulo $7$:

$$
3^1\equiv 3 \pmod 7,
$$

$$
3^2\equiv 9\equiv 2 \pmod 7,
$$

$$
3^3\equiv 6 \pmod 7,
$$

$$
3^4\equiv 18\equiv 4 \pmod 7,
$$

$$
3^5\equiv 12\equiv 5 \pmod 7,
$$

$$
3^6\equiv 15\equiv 1 \pmod 7.
$$

The first exponent that gives $1$ is $6$.

Therefore,

$$
\operatorname{ord}_7(3)=6.
$$

### Example 2

> Find $\operatorname{ord}_{15}(2)$.

Since

$$
\gcd(2,15)=1,
$$

the order exists.

Now compute:

$$
2^1\equiv 2 \pmod{15},
$$

$$
2^2\equiv 4 \pmod{15},
$$

$$
2^3\equiv 8 \pmod{15},
$$

$$
2^4\equiv 16\equiv 1 \pmod{15}.
$$

Thus

$$
\operatorname{ord}_{15}(2)=4.
$$

### Example 3

> Find the remainder when $5^{2026}$ is divided by $13$.

First find the order of $5$ modulo $13$.

Compute:

$$
5^1\equiv 5 \pmod{13},
$$

$$
5^2\equiv 25\equiv -1 \pmod{13}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
5^4\equiv 1 \pmod{13}.
$$

Since $5^2\not\equiv 1 \pmod{13}$, the order is

$$
\operatorname{ord}_{13}(5)=4.
$$

Now reduce the exponent:

$$
2026\equiv 2 \pmod 4.
$$

So

$$
5^{2026}\equiv 5^2\equiv -1 \equiv 12 \pmod{13}.
$$

The remainder is

$$
12.
$$

## Common Use in Contest Problems

Order is especially useful when a problem involves:
- Large powers modulo $n$
- Divisibility of expressions like $a^m-1$
- Repeating units digits
- Remainders with prime moduli
- Equations involving exponents

For example, if

$$
a^m\equiv 1 \pmod n,
$$

then

$$
\operatorname{ord}_n(a)\mid m.
$$

This often turns a modular equation into a divisibility condition.

## Key Contest Insight

The order of $a$ modulo $n$ is the period of the powers of $a$ modulo $n$.

Once you know the order, large exponents can be reduced modulo that order.

The most important fact is

$$
a^m\equiv 1 \pmod n
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\operatorname{ord}_n(a)\mid m.
$$

## Strategy Tips

- Always check that $\gcd(a,n)=1$
- For prime moduli, remember that the order divides $p-1$
- For general moduli, the order divides $\varphi(n)$
- Test divisors of $\varphi(n)$ from smallest to largest
- Once the order is known, reduce exponents modulo the order
- If the exponent leaves remainder $0$, use the power corresponding to the full
  cycle

## Common Pitfalls

- Forgetting that the order may not exist if $\gcd(a,n)\neq 1$
- Assuming the order is always $\varphi(n)$
- Testing exponents randomly instead of using divisors of $\varphi(n)$
- Reducing the base incorrectly
- Forgetting that the order is the smallest positive exponent giving $1$
