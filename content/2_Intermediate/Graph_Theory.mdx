## Overview

Graph theory studies relationships between objects.

A graph consists of vertices joined by edges. The vertices represent objects,
and the edges represent connections between them.

For example:
- People can be vertices, with friendships as edges
- Cities can be vertices, with roads as edges
- Rooms can be vertices, with doors as edges
- Numbers can be vertices, with edges joining numbers that satisfy a condition

Graph theory appears often in olympiad and contest problems because many
problems about movement, pairing, networks, games, and arrangements can be
translated into graph language.

In AMC style problems, a particularly common type asks about walks:

> How many ways can we move through a graph in exactly $n$ steps?

This module gives the basic vocabulary of graph theory and then spends extra
time on walk counting strategies.

## What Is a Graph?

A graph is made of two parts:
- A set of vertices
- A set of edges joining pairs of vertices

Formally, a graph is written as

$$
G=(V,E),
$$

where $V$ is the vertex set and $E$ is the edge set.

For example, suppose

$$
V=\{A,B,C,D\}
$$

and

$$
E=\{\{A,B\},\{B,C\},\{C,D\},\{D,A\}\}.
$$

This graph has four vertices and four edges. It forms a cycle:

$$
A-B-C-D-A.
$$

In this module, unless otherwise stated, graphs are simple and undirected.

That means:
- There is at most one edge between any two vertices
- No edge joins a vertex to itself
- Edges have no direction

A simple undirected graph is the most common type in introductory contest
problems.

## Vertices and Edges

The points of a graph are called vertices.

The connections between vertices are called edges.

If an edge joins vertices $u$ and $v$, we may write it as

$$
\{u,v\}.
$$

Since the graph is undirected,

$$
\{u,v\}=\{v,u\}.
$$

If there is an edge between $u$ and $v$, we say that $u$ and $v$ are adjacent.

If a vertex $v$ lies on an edge $e$, we say that $v$ is incident to $e$.

## Degree of a Vertex

The degree of a vertex is the number of edges incident to it.

The degree of a vertex $v$ is written as

$$
d(v).
$$

For example, if vertex $A$ is joined to $B$, $C$, and $D$, then

$$
d(A)=3.
$$

A vertex of degree $0$ is called an isolated vertex.

## The Handshaking Lemma

In any finite undirected graph,

$$
\sum_{v\in V}d(v)=2|E|.
$$

This is called the Handshaking Lemma.

It is true because every edge contributes $1$ to the degree of each of its two
endpoints. Therefore, every edge is counted exactly twice in the degree sum.

### Consequence

The number of vertices with odd degree is even.

Why?

The sum of all degrees is even, since it equals

$$
2|E|.
$$

A sum of integers is even only if it contains an even number of odd terms.

So every graph has an even number of odd degree vertices.

This simple fact is surprisingly useful in contest problems.

## Subgraphs

A graph $H$ is a subgraph of $G$ if its vertices and edges are taken from $G$.

If

$$
G=(V,E)
$$

and

$$
H=(V_H,E_H),
$$

then $H$ is a subgraph of $G$ when

$$
V_H\subseteq V
$$

and

$$
E_H\subseteq E.
$$

An induced subgraph is formed by choosing some vertices and keeping every edge
from the original graph whose endpoints are both among those chosen vertices.

For example, if a graph has vertices $A,B,C,D$ and we choose only $A,B,C$, then
the induced subgraph on $A,B,C$ contains all edges among $A,B,C$ that were
already present in the original graph.

## Complete Graphs

A complete graph is a graph in which every pair of distinct vertices is joined
by an edge.

The complete graph on $n$ vertices is denoted

$$
K_n.
$$

Since every edge is determined by choosing $2$ vertices from $n$, the number of
edges in $K_n$ is

$$
\binom{n}{2}
=
\frac{n(n-1)}{2}.
$$

For example,

$$
K_4
$$

has

$$
\binom{4}{2}=6
$$

edges.

A complete subgraph is often called a clique.

## Null Graphs and Independent Sets

A null graph is a graph with vertices but no edges.

Equivalently, every vertex is isolated.

An independent set in a graph is a set of vertices with no edges between any two
of them.

For example, if no two selected people are friends, then those selected people
form an independent set in the friendship graph.

Independent sets are closely related to complements of graphs.

## Complement of a Graph

The complement of a graph $G$ is the graph with the same vertex set, but with
exactly the missing edges.

In other words, two vertices are adjacent in the complement when they
are not adjacent in the original graph.

If $G$ has $n$ vertices and $e$ edges, then its complement has

$$
\binom{n}{2}-e
$$

edges.

This is useful because sometimes it is easier to count missing edges than
present edges.

## Connected Graphs

A graph is connected if every vertex can be reached from every other vertex by
following edges.

If a graph is not connected, it breaks into connected components.

A connected component is a largest possible connected subgraph.

For example, a graph made of two separate triangles has two connected
components.

## Directed Graphs

A directed graph, or digraph, has directed edges.

A directed edge from $u$ to $v$ is written as

$$
(u,v).
$$

This is different from

$$
(v,u).
$$

Directed graphs are useful for modelling one way roads, tournament results, or
processes with allowed directions.

A directed graph is strongly connected if every vertex can be reached from every
other vertex by following directed edges in the correct direction.

## Weighted Graphs

A weighted graph is a graph whose edges have weights.

The weights may represent:
- Distances
- Costs
- Times
- Probabilities
- Scores etc.

For example, a road map can be modelled by a weighted graph where cities are
vertices and road distances are edge weights.

Shortest path problems often use weighted graphs.

## Bipartite Graphs

A graph is bipartite if its vertices can be split into two sets, say $L$ and
$R$, such that every edge connects a vertex in $L$ to a vertex in $R$.

There are no edges within $L$, and no edges within $R$.

A graph is bipartite if and only if it has no odd cycle.

Equivalently, a graph is bipartite if and only if its vertices can be coloured
using two colours so that adjacent vertices always have different colours.

### Complete Bipartite Graphs

The complete bipartite graph

$$
K_{m,n}
$$

has two parts of sizes $m$ and $n$, and every vertex in the first part is joined
to every vertex in the second part.

It has

$$
mn
$$

edges.

## Planar Graphs

A graph is planar if it can be drawn in the plane so that no edges cross except
at vertices.

For example,

$$
K_4
$$

is planar, but

$$
K_5
$$

is not planar.

Also,

$$
K_{3,3}
$$

is not planar.

A planar drawing divides the plane into regions called faces. The outside
region is also counted as a face.

## Euler's Formula for Planar Graphs

For a connected planar graph with

$$
V
$$

vertices,

$$
E
$$

edges, and

$$
F
$$

faces, Euler's formula says

$$
V-E+F=2.
$$

This is one of the main results about planar graphs.

For a connected simple planar graph with

$$
V\ge 3,
$$

we also have

$$
E\le 3V-6.
$$

If the graph is triangle free and planar, then

$$
E\le 2V-4.
$$

These inequalities are often used to prove that certain graphs are not planar.

## Walks, Trails, Paths, and Cycles

This is one of the most important sections for contest problem solving.

A walk is a sequence of vertices where each consecutive pair is joined by an
edge.

Vertices and edges may repeat in a walk.

For example,

$$
A,B,C,B,A
$$

is a walk if all the needed edges exist.

A trail is a walk that does not repeat any edge.

A path is a walk that does not repeat any vertex.

A closed walk starts and ends at the same vertex.

A cycle is a closed path, meaning it starts and ends at the same vertex and does
not repeat other vertices.

These words are similar, so it is important to distinguish them carefully.

## Walk Length

The length of a walk is the number of edges used.

For example,

$$
A,B,C,D
$$

has length $3$, because it uses the edges

$$
AB,\quad BC,\quad CD.
$$

A walk of length $n$ has $n$ steps.

AMC style questions often ask:

> How many walks of length $n$ go from one vertex to another?

This means we count sequences of moves, not just simple paths.

Repeating vertices is allowed unless the problem says otherwise.

## The Main Walk Counting Idea

When counting walks, the most important question is:

> Where can I be after each step?

A walk counting problem is often solved by tracking the number of ways to be at
each type of vertex after each move.

This is dynamic programming on a graph.

Instead of listing every walk, we update counts step by step.

## Walks on a Small Graph

Suppose a graph has vertices

$$
A,B,C
$$

and edges

$$
AB,\quad BC.
$$

This is a path of length $2$:

$$
A-B-C.
$$

How many walks of length $4$ start at $A$ and end at $A$?

Let

$$
a_n
$$

be the number of ways to be at $A$ after $n$ steps,

$$
b_n
$$

be the number of ways to be at $B$ after $n$ steps,

and

$$
c_n
$$

be the number of ways to be at $C$ after $n$ steps.

Initially,

$$
a_0=1,\qquad b_0=0,\qquad c_0=0.
$$

From $A$, we can only move to $B$.

From $B$, we can move to $A$ or $C$.

From $C$, we can only move to $B$.

Therefore,

$$
a_{n+1}=b_n,
$$

$$
b_{n+1}=a_n+c_n,
$$

and

$$
c_{n+1}=b_n.
$$

Now work out:

After $1$ step,

$$
(a_1,b_1,c_1)=(0,1,0).
$$

After $2$ steps,

$$
(a_2,b_2,c_2)=(1,0,1).
$$

After $3$ steps,

$$
(a_3,b_3,c_3)=(0,2,0).
$$

After $4$ steps,

$$
(a_4,b_4,c_4)=(2,0,2).
$$

So there are

$$
2
$$

walks of length $4$ from $A$ back to $A$.

They are

$$
A-B-A-B-A
$$

and

$$
A-B-C-B-A.
$$

## Walks on a Square

Consider a square with vertices

$$
A,B,C,D
$$

and edges

$$
AB,\quad BC,\quad CD,\quad DA.
$$

How many walks of length $6$ start at $A$ and end at $A$?

A direct listing would be annoying, but symmetry makes the problem simple.

At any step, relative to $A$, a walker can be:
- At $A$
- At the opposite vertex $C$
- At one of the two adjacent vertices $B$ or $D$

Let

$$
x_n
$$

be the number of ways to be at $A$ after $n$ steps.

Let

$$
y_n
$$

be the total number of ways to be at $B$ or $D$ after $n$ steps.

Let

$$
z_n
$$

be the number of ways to be at $C$ after $n$ steps.

Initially,

$$
(x_0,y_0,z_0)=(1,0,0).
$$

From $A$, a move goes to one of $B,D$.

From $C$, a move also goes to one of $B,D$.

From $B$ or $D$, a move goes either to $A$ or to $C$.

As such,

$$
x_{n+1}=y_n,
$$

$$
y_{n+1}=2x_n+2z_n,
$$

and

$$
z_{n+1}=y_n.
$$

Now work out:

$$
(x_1,y_1,z_1)=(0,2,0)
$$

$$
(x_2,y_2,z_2)=(2,0,2)
$$

$$
(x_3,y_3,z_3)=(0,8,0)
$$

$$
(x_4,y_4,z_4)=(8,0,8)
$$

$$
(x_5,y_5,z_5)=(0,32,0)
$$

$$
(x_6,y_6,z_6)=(32,0,32).
$$

Therefore, there are

$$
32
$$

walks of length $6$ from $A$ back to $A$.

## Bipartite Graphs and Parity of Walks

Parity is extremely important in walk problems.

In a bipartite graph, every move switches sides.

If a walk starts in $L$, then:
- After an odd number of steps, it is in $R$
- After an even number of steps, it is in $L$

Therefore, in a bipartite graph, there is no odd length closed walk.

This gives fast zero answers.

### Example

A square is bipartite.

Therefore, the number of walks of length $7$ from a vertex back to itself is

$$
0.
$$

The walker would need to return to the same side after an odd number of moves,
which is impossible.

## Walks on Complete Graphs

Complete graphs also appear in counting problems.

In

$$
K_m,
$$

each vertex is connected to every other vertex, but not to itself.

Suppose we want the number of walks of length $n$ from a fixed vertex back to
itself.

Let

$$
a_n
$$

be the number of walks of length $n$ ending at the starting vertex.

Let

$$
b_n
$$

be the total number of walks of length $n$ ending at any other vertex.

Initially,

$$
a_0=1,
\qquad
b_0=0.
$$

To return to the starting vertex at step $n+1$, we must have been at another
vertex at step $n$:

$$
a_{n+1}=b_n.
$$

To end at a different vertex at step $n+1$:
- From the starting vertex, there are $m-1$ choices
- From a different vertex, there are $m-2$ choices that still end at a different
  vertex

Therefore,

$$
b_{n+1}=(m-1)a_n+(m-2)b_n.
$$

This recurrence solves many complete graph walk problems quickly.

### Example

In

$$
K_4,
$$

how many walks of length $4$ start and end at a fixed vertex?

Here,

$$
m=4.
$$

So

$$
a_{n+1}=b_n
$$

and

$$
b_{n+1}=3a_n+2b_n.
$$

Start with

$$
(a_0,b_0)=(1,0).
$$

Then

$$
(a_1,b_1)=(0,3)
$$

$$
(a_2,b_2)=(3,6)
$$

$$
(a_3,b_3)=(6,21)
$$

$$
(a_4,b_4)=(21,60).
$$

So the number of walks of length $4$ from a vertex back to itself is

$$
21.
$$

## Walks Using an Adjacency Matrix

For a graph with vertices

$$
v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n,
$$

the adjacency matrix is the matrix $A$ where the entry in row $i$, column $j$
is

$$
1
$$

if $v_i$ is joined to $v_j$, and

$$
0
$$

otherwise.

A useful result is:

> The entry in row $i$, column $j$ of $A^k$ is the number of walks of length
> $k$ from $v_i$ to $v_j$.

This is a very useful method when the graph is small.

## Why the Adjacency Matrix Method Works

The entry in row $i$, column $j$ of $A^2$ counts the number of ways to go from
$v_i$ to $v_j$ in two steps.

In turn, a two step walk has the form

$$
v_i\to v_r\to v_j.
$$

For each possible middle vertex $v_r$, we need both:

$$
v_i\to v_r
$$

and

$$
v_r\to v_j.
$$

This is what matrix multiplication counts.

Similarly, $A^3$ counts three step walks, and in general $A^k$ counts
$k$ step walks.

## Example Using an Adjacency Matrix

Consider the path graph

$$
A-B-C.
$$

Using the vertex order

$$
A,B,C,
$$

the adjacency matrix is

$$
M=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 & 0 \\
1 & 0 & 1 \\
0 & 1 & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Then

$$
M^2=
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 & 1 \\
0 & 2 & 0 \\
1 & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The entry in row $A$, column $C$ is $1$, so there is exactly one walk of length
$2$ from $A$ to $C$:

$$
A-B-C.
$$

The entry in row $B$, column $B$ is $2$, so there are two walks of length $2$
from $B$ back to $B$:

$$
B-A-B
$$

and

$$
B-C-B.
$$

## Walks vs. Paths

In contest problems, do not confuse walks and paths.

A walk may repeat vertices and edges.

A path may not repeat vertices.

For example, in the graph

$$
A-B-C,
$$

the sequence

$$
A-B-A-B-C
$$

is a walk from $A$ to $C$.

It is not a path, because it repeats vertices and edges.

If a problem asks for 'routes', 'ways to move', or 'walks', repetition is often
allowed unless the problem says otherwise.

If a problem asks for 'paths' or says 'without visiting a vertex more than
once', then repetition is not allowed.

## Euler Trails

An Euler trail is a trail that uses every edge of a graph exactly once.

An Euler circuit is an Euler trail that starts and ends at the same vertex.

For a connected graph with at least one edge:
- An Euler circuit exists if and only if every vertex has even degree
- An Euler trail that is not a circuit exists if and only if exactly two
  vertices have odd degree

The two odd degree vertices, if they exist, must be the start and end of the
Euler trail.

This is another direct application of parity.

## Trees

A tree is a connected graph with no cycles.

A forest is a graph with no cycles. Each connected component of a forest is a
tree.

For a tree with $n$ vertices, the number of edges is

$$
n-1.
$$

Conversely, a connected graph with $n$ vertices and $n-1$ edges is a tree.

Trees are important because there is exactly one path between any two vertices
in a tree.

## Leaves in Trees

A leaf is a vertex of degree $1$.

Every tree with at least two vertices has at least two leaves.

This is often useful in induction arguments and graph counting problems.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1

> A graph has $8$ vertices and $13$ edges. What is the sum of the degrees of
> all vertices?

By the Handshaking Lemma,

$$
\sum_{v\in V}d(v)=2|E|.
$$

Since

$$
|E|=13,
$$

the sum of the degrees is

$$
2(13)=26.
$$

### Example 2

> Can a graph have exactly three vertices of odd degree?

No.

The number of odd degree vertices in any graph must be even.

Therefore a graph cannot have exactly three odd degree vertices.

### Example 3

> How many edges does $K_{10}$ have?

A complete graph on $10$ vertices has one edge for each pair of vertices.

So the number of edges is

$$
\binom{10}{2}
=
45.
$$

### Example 4

> How many edges does $K_{3,5}$ have?

In a complete bipartite graph, every vertex in one part is joined to every
vertex in the other part.

So

$$
K_{3,5}
$$

has

$$
3(5)=15
$$

edges.

### Example 5

> A graph is bipartite. How many closed walks of odd length can it have?

It has none.

In a bipartite graph, each step switches sides. After an odd number of steps, a
walk must be on the opposite side from where it began.

So it cannot be closed.

### Example 6

> Consider the triangle graph $K_3$. How many walks of length $4$ start and end
> at a fixed vertex?

Use the complete graph recurrence with

$$
m=3.
$$

Let $a_n$ be the number of walks ending at the starting vertex after $n$ steps,
and $b_n$ the total number of walks ending at one of the other two vertices.

Then

$$
a_{n+1}=b_n
$$

and

$$
b_{n+1}=2a_n+b_n.
$$

Start with

$$
(a_0,b_0)=(1,0).
$$

Then

$$
(a_1,b_1)=(0,2)
$$

$$
(a_2,b_2)=(2,2)
$$

$$
(a_3,b_3)=(2,6)
$$

$$
(a_4,b_4)=(6,10).
$$

So there are

$$
6
$$

walks of length $4$ from a vertex back to itself.

### Example 7

> A square has vertices $A,B,C,D$ in order. How many walks of length $5$ go
> from $A$ to $C$?

A square is bipartite.

Vertices $A$ and $C$ are in the same part because they are opposite vertices.

After an odd number of steps, a walk starting at $A$ must be in the other part.

Since $5$ is odd, it is impossible to end at $C$.

Therefore, the number of walks is

$$
0.
$$

### Example 8

> A square has vertices $A,B,C,D$ in order. How many walks of length $4$ go
> from $A$ to $C$?

Use the same symmetry notation as before:
- $x_n$ is the number of walks ending at $A$
- $y_n$ is the total number ending at $B$ or $D$
- $z_n$ is the number ending at $C$

Initially,

$$
(x_0,y_0,z_0)=(1,0,0).
$$

The recurrences are

$$
x_{n+1}=y_n,
$$

$$
y_{n+1}=2x_n+2z_n,
$$

and

$$
z_{n+1}=y_n.
$$

Work out:

$$
(x_1,y_1,z_1)=(0,2,0)
$$

$$
(x_2,y_2,z_2)=(2,0,2)
$$

$$
(x_3,y_3,z_3)=(0,8,0)
$$

$$
(x_4,y_4,z_4)=(8,0,8).
$$

So the number of walks of length $4$ from $A$ to $C$ is

$$
8.
$$

## Problem Solving Methods for Walk Questions

Walk questions can look different, but most are solved using a small set of
methods.

### Method 1: Track Each Vertex

If the graph is small and asymmetric, keep a separate count for each vertex
after each step.

This is the safest method.

Let

$$
a_n,b_n,c_n,\ldots
$$

be the number of ways to be at each vertex after $n$ steps.

Then update using the edges of the graph.

### Method 2: Use Symmetry

If several vertices behave the same way, group them together.

For example, in a square starting at $A$, vertices $B$ and $D$ are symmetric.
So instead of tracking $B$ and $D$ separately, we can track the total number of
ways to be at either of them.

This makes the recurrence much shorter.

### Method 3: Use Parity

If the graph is bipartite, parity often gives an immediate answer.

A walk switches sides every step.

So:
- Same side requires an even number of steps
- Opposite side requires an odd number of steps

This can quickly show that the answer is $0$.

### Method 4: Use an Adjacency Matrix

If the graph has a small number of vertices and the walk length is not too
large, form the adjacency matrix.

Then entries of powers of the adjacency matrix count walks.

This method is systematic and works especially well when the graph is fixed.

### Method 5: Use Complementary States

Sometimes the exact vertex does not matter. What matters is whether we are:
- At the starting vertex
- At a neighbouring vertex
- At a non neighbouring vertex
- In a certain region

This is common in complete graphs, cubes, regular polygons, and symmetric
networks.

## Common AMC Style Walk Patterns

AMC 10 and AMC 12 walk problems often use small graphs and a fixed number of
steps.

The most common patterns are:
- Moving around a polygon
- Moving on a small network of points
- Walking on the edges of a cube
- Counting routes with exactly $n$ moves
- Returning to the starting point
- Reaching an opposite vertex
- Using parity to eliminate impossible cases

The safest approach is to avoid listing all walks. Instead, track the number of
ways to be in each state after each step.

## Key Contest Insight

Graph theory problems often become much easier after translating the problem
into vertices and edges.

For walk counting problems, remember:
- A walk may repeat vertices and edges
- Count by number of steps
- Update possible positions after each step
- Use symmetry whenever possible
- Use bipartite parity to detect impossible walks

The question is rarely about drawing an actual good looking graph. It is usually about
choosing the right states to track.

## Strategy Tips
- Identify the vertices and edges clearly
- Decide whether the graph is directed or undirected
- Check whether the graph is bipartite
- For walk problems, determine whether repetition is allowed
- Track positions after each step instead of listing walks
- Use symmetry to combine equivalent vertices
- For complete graphs, use a recurrence involving the starting vertex and all
  other vertices
- For small graphs, consider the adjacency matrix method
- For Euler trail problems, count odd degree vertices
- For tree problems, use the facts that a tree has no cycles and has $n-1$
  edges

## Common Pitfalls
- Confusing walks with paths
- Forgetting that walks may repeat vertices and edges
- Counting paths when the problem asks for walks
- Ignoring parity in bipartite graphs
- Forgetting that a closed walk starts and ends at the same vertex
- Assuming a graph is connected without checking
- Forgetting that the degree sum is twice the number of edges
- Using Euler trail conditions without checking connectedness
- Treating directed edges as undirected
- Counting rotations or symmetries incorrectly when the graph itself has
  symmetry
