## Strategy for any timed contest

Everything in this first section applies to any timed contest, in any country.

Before the day, first make sure you know the actual format of the contest. How
many problems are there? How much time do you get? How is it scored, and do
wrong answers lose points? It might seem like a waste of time to look all of
this up, but two contests with the same problems and different scoring rules
would always be taken quite differently.

During the contest, first skim the entire test paper. Problems aren't always
in order of difficulty, and there can be deceptively easy ones sitting right at
the end, which you'd never get to if you went strictly in order. If you've been
stuck on a problem for a few minutes, mark it and move on, since you can always
come back to it later.

For multiple-choice questions, if you're stuck, try to cross out impossible
choices. Plugging answers back into the problem and checking whether they
give a valid answer is also a strategy that's fine to use. The most important
tip I'd give, though, is that you should always reread the question before
answering. It's almost a rite of passage to solve for $x$ when the question
had wanted $2x$, for example.

It's also quite important to get good sleep the night before. After all, your
brain isn't going to function properly without it. On test day, bring only
what's allowed, and arrive early so that you're not late.

## US formats

Test formats change, so check the
[MAA's website](https://maa.org/student-programs/amc/) just in case. Even the
AMC 8 and the AMC 10 score completely differently, despite being run by the
same people. If you compete somewhere else, look up your own contest's rules
and use the same reasoning.

### AMC 8

On the AMC 8, you get 40 minutes for 25 multiple-choice questions. That works
out to about a minute and a half per question, which goes by faster than you'd
think. There's no penalty for wrong answers, so never leave anything blank,
even if it's a complete guess.

### AMC 10 and AMC 12

Both are 25 multiple-choice questions in 75 minutes. You get 6 points for a
correct answer, 1.5 for a blank, and 0 for a wrong answer. With five choices
per question, whether a guess is worth it comes down to how many of them
you've ruled out:

| Choices left | Expected value of a guess | Versus a blank (1.5) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | $6/5 = 1.2$ | Worse |
| 4 | $6/4 = 1.5$ | Even |
| 3 | $6/3 = 2$ | Better |
| 2 | $6/2 = 3$ | Much better |

So guessing blind actually costs you points on average. Cross out two choices
first, and then it's worth it.

It's also better to be accurate than to rush through every problem. If you
answer 14 correctly and leave the other 11 blank, that's
$14 \cdot 6 + 11 \cdot 1.5 = 100.5$, which on the AMC 10 is already enough to
qualify for the AIME. In other words, **you can leave more than a third of the
test blank and still make the AIME.**

### AIME

On the AIME, you're given 3 hours for 15 problems. If you divide the time
equally, that's about 12 minutes each. Answers are integers from 0 to 999, and
there's no penalty for wrong answers, so always answer every question. Since
the answer space is so wide, you can't really confirm whether your answer
"makes sense" from meta-information (such as the choices in a multiple-choice
question). Thus, it's of utmost importance to always check your arithmetic
and make sure you aren't making any silly mistakes.

### USAJMO and USAMO

The USA(J)MO is two days long. Each day you get 3 proof-based problems and 4.5
hours to do them, and every problem is graded out of 7, with partial credit if
you've made any actual progress. Don't rush. Try to gain intuition for the
problems, and try to focus on one problem, since a full solution to one
problem is generally worth more than partial credit on all three. Also, leave
time to write up your solutions properly. Don't leave steps as "trivial, and
an exercise for the reader."
