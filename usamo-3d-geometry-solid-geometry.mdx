# Olympiad 3D Geometry & Solid Geometry

**Author:** Neal Shandilya

Solid geometry appears less often than planar geometry in olympiad problems. Most problems are handled by a small set of recurring reductions.

> **Do not think in three dimensions longer than necessary.**

Typical reductions include:

1. finding a useful **planar cross-section**;
2. using **orthogonal projection**;
3. exploiting **symmetry**;
4. choosing simple **coordinates or vectors**;
5. comparing **volumes**;
6. using an **affine normalization**.

Topics covered:

- lines and planes
- perpendicularity
- orthogonal projection
- planar cross-sections
- angles and dihedral angles
- vectors and coordinates
- distances in space
- tetrahedra
- spheres
- volume ratios
- affine geometry
- polyhedra

---

# Part I. Lines and Planes

## 1. Determining a Plane

A unique plane is determined by any of the following:

- three noncollinear points
- a line and a point not on the line
- two intersecting lines
- two distinct parallel lines

Three arbitrary points do **not** determine a unique plane if they are collinear.

---

## 2. Relative Positions of Lines

Two distinct lines in space may be:

- intersecting
- parallel
- skew

### Skew Lines

Two lines are **skew** if they:

- do not intersect
- are not parallel
- do not lie in a common plane

Nonintersecting lines are not necessarily parallel.

---

## 3. Relative Positions of Planes

Two distinct planes are either:

- parallel, or
- intersecting in a line

Two nonparallel planes cannot intersect in only one point.

---

# Part II. Perpendicularity

## 4. Line Perpendicular to a Plane

Suppose \(l\) is a line and \(\Pi\) is a plane.

If \(m,n\subset\Pi\) are two intersecting lines and

\[
l\perp m,
\qquad
l\perp n,
\]

then

\[
\boxed{l\perp\Pi}.
\]

Perpendicularity to a single line in \(\Pi\) is not enough.

---

## 5. Consequence of Perpendicularity to a Plane

If

\[
l\perp\Pi,
\]

then \(l\) is perpendicular to every line in \(\Pi\) passing through the point \(l\cap\Pi\).

---

## 6. Perpendicular Planes

If plane \(\Pi_1\) contains a line perpendicular to plane \(\Pi_2\), then

\[
\Pi_1\perp\Pi_2.
\]

---

## 7. The Three-Perpendiculars Principle

Let \(P\) project orthogonally to \(H\) in plane \(\Pi\).

Take \(A\in\Pi\), and suppose a line \(l\subset\Pi\) through \(A\) satisfies

\[
l\perp AH.
\]

Since

\[
PH\perp\Pi,
\]

we also have

\[
l\perp PH.
\]

Thus \(l\) is perpendicular to two intersecting lines \(AH\) and \(PH\) in plane \(APH\), so

\[
\boxed{l\perp AP}.
\]

The spatial perpendicularity has been reduced to a planar one.

---

# Part III. Orthogonal Projection

## 8. Projection of a Point onto a Plane

Let \(P\) lie outside plane \(\Pi\), and let \(H\) be the foot of the perpendicular from \(P\) to \(\Pi\).

For every \(X\in\Pi\),

\[
PX^2=PH^2+HX^2.
\]

Therefore

\[
PX\ge PH.
\]

So \(PH\) is the shortest distance from \(P\) to the plane.

---

## 9. Equidistance and Projection

Suppose \(A,B,C\) are noncollinear points in plane \(\Pi\), and

\[
PA=PB=PC.
\]

Let \(H\) be the projection of \(P\) onto \(\Pi\).

Then

\[
PA^2=PH^2+HA^2,
\]

and similarly

\[
PB^2=PH^2+HB^2,
\qquad
PC^2=PH^2+HC^2.
\]

Hence

\[
HA=HB=HC.
\]

Therefore \(H\) is the circumcenter of \(\triangle ABC\).

If a point outside a plane is equidistant from three noncollinear points in the plane, its orthogonal projection is their circumcenter.

---

## 10. Perpendicular-Bisector Planes

For two points \(A,B\), the set of all points \(P\) satisfying

\[
PA=PB
\]

is the plane perpendicular to \(AB\) through the midpoint of \(AB\).

Perpendicular-bisector planes help locate sphere centers.

---

# Part IV. Planar Cross-Sections

## 11. Choosing a Cross-Section

Many difficult-looking 3D configurations become ordinary Euclidean geometry after choosing the correct plane.

Common choices include planes through:

- the center of a sphere
- a tangent point
- an altitude
- an axis of symmetry
- a diagonal of a cube
- two relevant edges
- a point and its projection
- several relevant vertices of a tetrahedron

Before introducing coordinates, check whether the relevant objects lie in one plane.

---

## 12. Cross-Section of a Sphere

Suppose a plane cuts a sphere with center \(O\).

The intersection is a circle.

If \(H\) is the projection of \(O\) onto the plane, then \(H\) is the center of the intersection circle.

If the sphere has radius \(R\), the distance from \(O\) to the plane is \(d\), and the cross-section has radius \(r\), then

\[
R^2=d^2+r^2.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{r^2=R^2-d^2}.
\]

---

# Part V. Angles in Space

## 13. Angle Between Two Lines

For two intersecting lines, use the ordinary planar angle.

For two nonintersecting lines, translate one line parallel to itself until the two directions meet.

Thus the angle between two lines depends only on their direction vectors.

If their direction vectors are \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\),

\[
\cos\theta
=
\frac{|\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v|}
{|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|}.
\]

---

## 14. Angle Between a Line and a Plane

Let a line have direction vector \(\mathbf v\), and let the plane have normal vector \(\mathbf n\).

If \(\theta\) is the acute angle between the line and the plane,

\[
\boxed{
\sin\theta
=
\frac{|\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf n|}
{|\mathbf v||\mathbf n|}
}.
\]

Geometrically, the line-plane angle is the angle between the line and its orthogonal projection onto the plane.

---

## 15. Dihedral Angles

Suppose two planes \(\Pi_1,\Pi_2\) meet along line \(l\).

To measure the angle between the planes:

1. choose a point \(P\in l\)
2. draw \(m\subset\Pi_1\) through \(P\) with \(m\perp l\)
3. draw \(n\subset\Pi_2\) through \(P\) with \(n\perp l\)

Then the angle between \(m\) and \(n\) is the **dihedral angle** between the planes.

This converts a plane-plane angle into an ordinary planar angle.

---

## 16. Angle Between Two Planes Using Normals

If \(\mathbf n_1,\mathbf n_2\) are normal vectors to the planes, then their acute angle satisfies

\[
\boxed{
\cos\theta
=
\frac{|\mathbf n_1\cdot\mathbf n_2|}
{|\mathbf n_1||\mathbf n_2|}
}.
\]

The two possible dihedral angles are supplementary, so always check which one the problem asks for.

---

# Part VI. Vectors

## 17. Dot Product

For vectors \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\),

\[
\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v
=
|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|\cos\theta.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf u\perp\mathbf v
\iff
\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=0
}.
\]

The dot product gives a direct algebraic test for perpendicularity.

---

## 18. Equations of Planes

A plane through point \(P_0\) with normal vector \(\mathbf n\) has equation

\[
\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf p-\mathbf p_0)=0.
\]

In coordinates,

\[
ax+by+cz=d,
\]

with normal vector

\[
(a,b,c).
\]

---

## 19. Coplanarity

Four points \(A,B,C,D\) are coplanar exactly when

\[
\overrightarrow{AB},
\quad
\overrightarrow{AC},
\quad
\overrightarrow{AD}
\]

are linearly dependent.

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot
\left(
\overrightarrow{AC}\times\overrightarrow{AD}
\right)=0
}.
\]

---

# Part VII. Coordinates

## 20. Choosing Coordinates Well

Coordinates should reflect the geometry.

Avoid completely general coordinates unless absolutely necessary.

Good coordinates should make:

- perpendicular lines align with coordinate axes
- symmetric points have symmetric coordinates
- midpoints or centroids simple
- planes become equations like \(x=0\) or \(x+y+z=c\)

---

## 21. Rectangular Box

Let

\[
A=(0,0,0)
\]

and place the three mutually perpendicular edges through \(A\) along the coordinate axes.

If their lengths are \(a,b,c\), then the opposite vertex is

\[
G=(a,b,c).
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{AG=\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}.
\]

---

## 22. Cube

For a unit cube, use the eight points

\[
\{0,1\}^3.
\]

With these coordinates, perpendicularity and plane equations stay simple.

---

## 23. Regular Tetrahedron

A symmetric model is

\[
A=(1,1,1),
\]

\[
B=(1,-1,-1),
\]

\[
C=(-1,1,-1),
\]

\[
D=(-1,-1,1).
\]

Every edge has length

\[
2\sqrt2,
\]

and the centroid is

\[
(0,0,0).
\]

It works well for:

- opposite-edge problems
- perpendicularity
- distances
- centers
- dihedral angles

---

# Part VIII. Distances in Space

## 24. Point-to-Plane Distance

For plane

\[
ax+by+cz+d=0
\]

and point

\[
P=(x_0,y_0,z_0),
\]

the distance is

\[
\boxed{
\frac{|ax_0+by_0+cz_0+d|}
{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}
}.
\]

In olympiad solutions, projection or volume may give a shorter argument.

---

## 25. Distance Between Parallel Planes

If

\[
ax+by+cz=d_1
\]

and

\[
ax+by+cz=d_2,
\]

then their distance is

\[
\boxed{
\frac{|d_1-d_2|}
{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}
}.
\]

---

## 26. Distance Between Skew Lines

Suppose

\[
L_1:A+t\mathbf u,
\qquad
L_2:B+s\mathbf v,
\]

with \(\mathbf u\) not parallel to \(\mathbf v\).

Then

\[
\mathbf u\times\mathbf v
\]

is perpendicular to both lines.

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{dist}(L_1,L_2)
=
\frac{
|\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)|
}{
|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v|
}
}.
\]

In a synthetic solution, it may be easier to find the actual **common perpendicular**.

---

# Part IX. Spheres

## 27. Tangent Plane to a Sphere

If plane \(\Pi\) is tangent to a sphere at \(T\), and \(O\) is the sphere's center, then

\[
\boxed{OT\perp\Pi}.
\]

The statement is the spatial tangent-radius theorem.

---

## 28. Sphere Through Four Points

Four noncoplanar points determine a unique sphere.

For tetrahedron \(ABCD\), the sphere center \(O\) satisfies

\[
OA=OB=OC=OD.
\]

Let \(H\) be the circumcenter of \(\triangle ABC\).

Because

\[
OA=OB=OC,
\]

the projection of \(O\) onto plane \(ABC\) must be \(H\).

Hence \(O\) lies on the line through \(H\) perpendicular to plane \(ABC\).

---

## 29. Power of a Point

For a sphere with center \(O\) and radius \(R\),

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Pow}(P)=PO^2-R^2
}.
\]

If a line through \(P\) meets the sphere at \(A,B\), then

\[
\boxed{
PA\cdot PB=PO^2-R^2
}.
\]

Take the plane through \(P,O,A,B\) and apply the ordinary power-of-a-point theorem.

---

## 30. Radical Plane

For two spheres, the set of points having equal powers to the spheres is a plane.

This plane is the **radical plane**.

If the sphere centers are \(O_1,O_2\), then the radical plane is perpendicular to

\[
O_1O_2.
\]

---

## 31. Radical Axis of Three Spheres

Suppose three spheres have pairwise radical planes.

If two radical planes intersect in a line \(l\), then every point on \(l\) has equal power to all three spheres.

Therefore \(l\) lies in the third radical plane.

The same equal-power argument used for radical centers in the plane applies here.

---

# Part X. Tetrahedra

## 32. Volume

For tetrahedron \(ABCD\),

\[
\boxed{
V=\frac13[\triangle ABC]h_D
},
\]

where \(h_D\) is the perpendicular distance from \(D\) to plane \(ABC\).

Using vectors,

\[
\boxed{
V=
\frac16
\left|
\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot
\left(
\overrightarrow{AC}\times
\overrightarrow{AD}
\right)
\right|
}.
\]

---

## 33. Volume Ratios

If two tetrahedra have the same base,

\[
\frac{V_1}{V_2}
=
\frac{h_1}{h_2}.
\]

If they have the same altitude and their bases lie in one plane,

\[
\frac{V_1}{V_2}
=
\frac{A_1}{A_2}.
\]

Volume ratios play the role of area ratios in many tetrahedron problems.

---

## 34. Barycentric Interpretation of Volume

Let \(P\) lie inside tetrahedron \(ABCD\).

Then

\[
[PBCD],
\quad
[PACD],
\quad
[PABD],
\quad
[PABC]
\]

determine the barycentric coordinates of \(P\).

For example, if all four volumes are equal, then

\[
P=\frac{A+B+C+D}{4}.
\]

Thus \(P\) is the centroid.

---

## 35. Centroid

For tetrahedron \(ABCD\),

\[
\boxed{
G=\frac{A+B+C+D}{4}
}.
\]

Let \(M\) be the centroid of face \(BCD\). Then

\[
M=\frac{B+C+D}{3}.
\]

Therefore

\[
G
=
\frac14A+\frac34M.
\]

So \(G\) lies on \(AM\), with

\[
\boxed{AG:GM=3:1}.
\]

---

## 36. Incenter

A point inside a tetrahedron that has equal perpendicular distance from all four faces is the **incenter**.

If that common distance is \(r\), then \(r\) is the inradius.

Splitting the tetrahedron into four smaller tetrahedra gives

\[
V
=
\frac r3
(S_1+S_2+S_3+S_4),
\]

where \(S_1,S_2,S_3,S_4\) are the face areas.

Thus

\[
\boxed{
V=\frac{rS}{3}
},
\]

where \(S\) is the total surface area.

---

# Part XI. Regular Tetrahedron

## 37. Basic Formulas

Let a regular tetrahedron have side length \(a\).

Its altitude is

\[
\boxed{
h=a\sqrt{\frac23}
}.
\]

Its volume is

\[
\boxed{
V=\frac{a^3}{6\sqrt2}
}.
\]

Its circumradius is

\[
\boxed{
R=\frac{\sqrt6}{4}a
}.
\]

Its inradius is

\[
\boxed{
r=\frac{\sqrt6}{12}a
}.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{R=3r}.
\]

---

## 38. Opposite Edges

Let \(ABCD\) be a regular tetrahedron.

Let \(M,N\) be the midpoints of opposite edges \(AB,CD\).

Then

\[
MN\perp AB,
\qquad
MN\perp CD.
\]

Furthermore,

\[
\boxed{
MN=\frac{a}{\sqrt2}
}.
\]

Thus \(MN\) is the common perpendicular between the opposite edges.

---

## 39. Dihedral Angle of a Regular Tetrahedron

Let \(\theta\) be the acute dihedral angle between two faces of a regular tetrahedron.

Then

\[
\boxed{
\cos\theta=\frac13
}.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\theta=\arccos\frac13
}.
\]

This is approximately

\[
70.53^\circ.
\]

---

# Part XII. Orthocentric Tetrahedra

## 40. A Fundamental Opposite-Edge Theorem

Suppose tetrahedron \(ABCD\) satisfies

\[
AB\perp CD
\]

and

\[
AC\perp BD.
\]

Then automatically

\[
\boxed{AD\perp BC}.
\]

### Vector Proof

Set

\[
\mathbf b=\overrightarrow{AB},
\qquad
\mathbf c=\overrightarrow{AC},
\qquad
\mathbf d=\overrightarrow{AD}.
\]

From

\[
AB\perp CD,
\]

we obtain

\[
\mathbf b\cdot(\mathbf d-\mathbf c)=0.
\]

Thus

\[
\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf d
=
\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf c.
\]

Similarly,

\[
AC\perp BD
\]

gives

\[
\mathbf c\cdot(\mathbf d-\mathbf b)=0,
\]

so

\[
\mathbf c\cdot\mathbf d
=
\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf c.
\]

Therefore

\[
\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf c
=
\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf b,
\]

and hence

\[
\mathbf d\cdot(\mathbf c-\mathbf b)=0.
\]

But \(\mathbf c-\mathbf b\) is a direction vector of \(BC\).

Thus

\[
AD\perp BC.
\]

---

## 41. Orthocentric Tetrahedron

A tetrahedron whose altitudes are concurrent is called **orthocentric**.

A tetrahedron is orthocentric exactly when its pairs of opposite edges are perpendicular.


---

# Part XIII. Affine Geometry

## 42. Affine Transformations

Affine transformations preserve:

- collinearity
- coplanarity
- parallelism
- ratios along a line
- midpoints
- centroids
- area ratios
- volume ratios

They do **not** preserve:

- lengths
- angles
- perpendicularity
- circles
- spheres

---

## 43. Affine Normalization of a Tetrahedron

If a tetrahedron problem depends only on incidence, parallelism, ratios, or volumes, normalize to

\[
A=(0,0,0),
\]

\[
B=(1,0,0),
\]

\[
C=(0,1,0),
\]

\[
D=(0,0,1).
\]

The normalized tetrahedron is easier to calculate with.

Affine normalization does not preserve the following data:

- equal lengths
- perpendicularity
- angles
- circles or spheres

---

# Part XIV. Polyhedra

## 44. Euler's Formula

For a convex polyhedron,

\[
\boxed{V-E+F=2},
\]

where \(V,E,F\) denote the numbers of vertices, edges, and faces.

If every face is a \(k\)-gon,

\[
kF=2E.
\]

If every vertex has degree \(d\),

\[
dV=2E.
\]

Together with Euler's formula, these relations constrain regular and semiregular structures.

---

# Part XV. Worked Examples

## Example 1: Projection and Circumcenters

Point \(P\) lies outside plane \(ABC\), and

\[
PA=PB=PC.
\]

Prove that the line joining \(P\) to the circumcenter \(O\) of triangle \(ABC\) is perpendicular to plane \(ABC\).

### Solution

Let \(H\) be the orthogonal projection of \(P\) onto plane \(ABC\).

Then

\[
PA^2=PH^2+HA^2,
\]

\[
PB^2=PH^2+HB^2,
\]

\[
PC^2=PH^2+HC^2.
\]

Since

\[
PA=PB=PC,
\]

we get

\[
HA=HB=HC.
\]

Thus \(H\) is the circumcenter of \(\triangle ABC\), so

\[
H=O.
\]

Since \(PH\perp ABC\),

\[
\boxed{PO\perp ABC}.
\]

---

## Example 2: Cube Perpendicularity

In cube \(ABCDEFGH\), prove that

\[
AG\perp BDE.
\]

### Solution

Use

\[
A=(0,0,0),
\quad
B=(1,0,0),
\]

\[
D=(0,1,0),
\quad
E=(0,0,1),
\]

\[
G=(1,1,1).
\]

Then

\[
\overrightarrow{AG}=(1,1,1).
\]

Inside plane \(BDE\),

\[
\overrightarrow{BD}=(-1,1,0),
\]

and

\[
\overrightarrow{BE}=(-1,0,1).
\]

Now

\[
(1,1,1)\cdot(-1,1,0)=0
\]

and

\[
(1,1,1)\cdot(-1,0,1)=0.
\]

Thus \(AG\) is perpendicular to two intersecting lines in plane \(BDE\).

Therefore

\[
\boxed{AG\perp BDE}.
\]

---

## Example 3: Sphere Cross-Section

A sphere has radius \(13\). A plane cuts it in a circle of radius \(12\).

Find the distance from the center to the plane.

### Solution

Let the distance be \(d\).

Then

\[
13^2=d^2+12^2.
\]

Hence

\[
d^2=169-144=25.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{d=5}.
\]

---

## Example 4: Opposite Edges of a Regular Tetrahedron

A regular tetrahedron has side length \(a\).

Find the distance between two opposite edges.

### Solution

Let \(M,N\) be their midpoints.

By symmetry,

\[
MN\perp AB
\]

and

\[
MN\perp CD.
\]

Thus \(MN\) is the common perpendicular.

Using the symmetric coordinate model, the model tetrahedron has edge length

\[
2\sqrt2
\]

and midpoint distance

\[
2.
\]

Therefore

\[
\frac{MN}{a}
=
\frac{2}{2\sqrt2}
=
\frac1{\sqrt2}.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
MN=\frac{a}{\sqrt2}
}.
\]

---

## Example 5: A Tri-Rectangular Tetrahedron

Suppose

\[
AB\perp AC,
\qquad
AB\perp AD,
\qquad
AC\perp AD,
\]

with

\[
AB=a,\qquad AC=b,\qquad AD=c.
\]

Find the area of face \(BCD\).

### Solution

Use

\[
A=(0,0,0),
\]

\[
B=(a,0,0),
\qquad
C=(0,b,0),
\qquad
D=(0,0,c).
\]

Then

\[
\overrightarrow{BC}=(-a,b,0),
\]

\[
\overrightarrow{BD}=(-a,0,c).
\]

Their cross product has magnitude

\[
\sqrt{
a^2b^2+a^2c^2+b^2c^2
}.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
[BCD]
=
\frac12
\sqrt{
a^2b^2+a^2c^2+b^2c^2
}
}.
\]

---

## Example 6: Equal Subvolumes

Point \(P\) lies inside tetrahedron \(ABCD\) and

\[
[PBCD]
=
[PACD]
=
[PABD]
=
[PABC].
\]

Prove that \(P\) is the centroid.

### Solution

The four tetrahedra partition \(ABCD\), so each has one-fourth of its volume.

Hence the barycentric coordinates of \(P\) are

\[
\frac14,\frac14,\frac14,\frac14.
\]

Therefore

\[
P
=
\frac{A+B+C+D}{4}.
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{P=G}.
\]

---

# Part XVI. Problem-Solving Strategy

Try the following reductions in order.

## Step 1. Reduce to 2D

Look for a plane containing:

- the relevant points
- a sphere center
- a tangent point
- a projection
- an altitude
- an axis of symmetry

Once the right cross-section is chosen, the remaining argument may be planar.

---

## Step 2. Try a Projection

Projection is worth checking when the configuration contains:

- equal distances
- perpendicular distances to a plane
- circumcenters
- spheres

---

## Step 3. Check Symmetry

For cubes and regular tetrahedra, symmetry can determine that:

- a line must pass through a midpoint
- a line must be perpendicular to an edge
- several distances are equal

Record the symmetric relations before introducing coordinates.

---

## Step 4. Choose Coordinates

Coordinates work well when:

- the figure is a cube or rectangular box
- three edges are mutually perpendicular
- the tetrahedron is regular
- the conclusion is perpendicularity or coplanarity

Use coordinates when they simplify the structure of the figure.

---

## Step 5. Use Volume for Distances

Remember

\[
V=\frac13Bh.
\]

If the volume and base area are known, the perpendicular distance follows from the volume formula.

---

## Step 6. Check Affine Invariance

If only the following matter:

- parallelism
- ratios
- centroids
- coplanarity
- volume ratios

then affine normalization may simplify everything.

---

## Step 7. Translate the Goal into Vectors

For perpendicularity:

\[
\boxed{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=0}.
\]

For coplanarity:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w)=0
}.
\]

For equal lengths:

\[
|\mathbf u|^2=|\mathbf v|^2.
\]

Squared lengths avoid unnecessary radicals.

---

# Part XVII. Common Errors

## 1. Trusting the Diagram

A 2D drawing of a 3D object does not preserve angles.

Something that appears perpendicular may not be.

---

## 2. Confusing Skew and Parallel Lines

Two lines can fail to intersect without being parallel.

Check coplanarity before calling two nonintersecting lines parallel.

---

## 3. Proving Perpendicularity to Only One Line

To prove

\[
l\perp\Pi,
\]

show that \(l\) is perpendicular to two intersecting lines in \(\Pi\).

---

## 4. Choosing General Coordinates

Coordinates such as

\[
A=(a_1,a_2,a_3),
\quad
B=(b_1,b_2,b_3)
\]

hide the structure of the problem.

Choose coordinates adapted to the symmetry.

---

## 5. Missing the Cross-Section

Many sphere and tetrahedron problems reduce to circle or triangle geometry in the right plane.

---

## 6. Using Affine Geometry Illegally

Affine transformations do not preserve angles or perpendicularity.

After an affine normalization, metric conclusions require separate justification.

---

## 7. Confusing Plane Angle and Dihedral Angle

Arbitrary lines chosen from two planes do not, in general, measure the dihedral angle.

Use lines perpendicular to their intersection line, or use plane normals.

---

# Part XVIII. Graded Problem Set

## Level I: Fundamentals

### Problem 1

A cube has side length \(a\).

Find its space diagonal.

\[

\qquad
l\perp n

\[
\boxed{l\perp\Pi}.
\]
